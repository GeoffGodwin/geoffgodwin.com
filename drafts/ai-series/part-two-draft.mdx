Part one argued that the interesting risk in these systems sits in the objective somebody wrote and the tools somebody granted, rather than in anything the model wants. That left a question hanging, so I want to pick that up in this edition. If the "thing" doesn't want anything, what is it, and what would it need to have before any of us should say it understands what it's doing?

Before getting to that, there's a piece of vocabulary that keeps tripping up this whole discourse online. Artificial intelligence is a field of study, about seventy years old, covering search and planning and knowledge representation and learning and perception and robotics, among other things.[^rn] Large language models are one product of one branch of the umbrella field that is Artificial Intelligence. The public conversation has conveniently collapsed the first into the second, so that "AI" now means "the chatbot," and a claim about the chatbot gets heard as a claim about the entire discipline.

That collapse happened because of how these things feel to use. When something answers you in fluent, responsive, context-aware prose, the inference that it understands you is close to involuntary. Joseph Weizenbaum documented this in 1966 with ELIZA, a pattern-matching script that mostly reflected your own statements back at you as questions, and what unsettled him was how readily people confided in it anyway.[^eliza] I'll call this the fluency error: mistaking a test of output quality for a test of mind. It's the engine underneath both of the wrong pictures from part one, because the doom camp and the dismissal camp are each reading the output and then inferring what must be behind it in a highly inductive manner.

## Giving the Other Side Its Due

The "it's just a word guessing machine" line is accurate about one thing, so I will give it that before I go into my critique. The pretraining objective really is next-word prediction, and the mechanism is about as plain as it sounds. Show the model a stretch of text with the next word hidden, let it guess, nudge it when it guesses wrong, and repeat that a few trillion times across most of the written internet. The GPT-2 paper describes that same process in the language of statistics, calling it "unsupervised distribution estimation."[^gpt2] That's a formal way of saying the model is learning the shape of how language tends to go, rather than being taught any particular fact about the world.

That line can be misleading though in what it implies about the trained system. Anthropic's interpretability team spent 2025 tracing circuits inside one of their production models, and two of the findings are fairly compelling. Ask one of these models for a rhyming couplet, and it turns out to plan ahead. Before writing a single word of the second line, it has already settled on the word it means to finish that line with, and it then writes the line to arrive there. The researchers knew this was real rather than a pattern they were reading into the data, because when they reached in and deleted the planned word, the model rewrote the whole line toward a different rhyme. They found this in about half the poems they looked at.[^circuits] Separately, there's a small model that was trained only to predict legal moves in the board game Othello. It turned out to be tracking the state of the board inside itself, which nobody trained it to do, and editing that internal picture changed the moves it went on to predict.[^othello]

Now I do want to be careful about how far those two findings go, because the secondhand versions of them tend to claim a good deal more than the papers do. The multilingual result is where I notice this most. Anthropic described the features in the middle layers as "more language-agnostic," and that's the half of the research that gets regularly quoted and regurgitated in the zietgeist. They also found that "there are important mechanistic ways in which English is privileged," and they describe a picture "in which English is the default output," which is the other half that doesn't. So to summarize, concepts are partially shared across languages with English sitting at the center, and that's a long way short of the language-independent thinking machine that the summaries imply.

The authors were also forthcoming about the limits of their own method, which I commend them for. Their tracing gives them real insight on roughly a quarter of the prompts they tried. The cases they chose to publish are a selected sample out of that quarter. And what they're offering is proof that a mechanism exists in a particular example, with no promise at all that the same mechanism shows up anywhere else.

The stochastic parrot position deserves the same courtesy, because it's a real academic argument rather than a slogan. Bender and colleagues wrote in reference to a language model that it is "a system for haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms it has observed in its vast training data, according to probabilistic information about how they combine, but without any reference to meaning."[^parrots] That's a claim about meaning rather than about ability, and I read that as a very strong statement. It's also the claim I'll come back to in the fourth property below. It's worth knowing, though, that the parrot argument is one section of a paper mostly concerned with environmental cost, training data documentation, and opportunity cost.

Here's the pivot, and it's why I've given both sides of the argument plenty of room. Neither the interpretability results nor the parrot argument touches the four things I'm about to describe. Both of those arguments are about what is or isn't happening inside the model. The four properties are about the model's relationship to time, to the world, and to whether anything is at stake for it, and you can settle every one of them without opening that magic box up.

## Four Properties

What follows is what I'd call a definition by absence. I'm not going to tell you what comprehension is, because I don't know, and I'm fairly suspicious of anyone who says they do. What I can do is name four properties that brains have, that current systems verifiably lack, and that no amount of scaffolding built around the model can solve. Each one starts by correcting something about us rather than about the machine. We tend to measure these systems against a flattering picture of how our own minds work, and that picture is usually wrong. Fix it first, and the gap that's left turns out to be narrower in some places than people expect, and a good deal wider in others.

### Continuous Learning

Let's start with the correction. Our (human) memory is a good deal worse than many realize. Working memory holds something like three to five items at a time, once you stop people from rehearsing them or grouping them together, rather than the famous seven.[^cowan] And what we pull back out later is rebuilt rather than replayed, which Loftus and Palmer showed by changing one verb in a question which resulted in a change to what people remembered experiencing.[^loftus] So we aren't comparing a perfect biological recorder against a lossy machine here; both sides lose things.

The real difference is what happens afterward. Brains compress experience into long-term structure, a lot of that work happening during sleep, so the version of you that wakes up tomorrow has been quietly reorganized by today.[^sleep] Neuroscientists call that consolidation, and it's the part that has no counterpart in today's silicon-based models. A deployed model's weights are frozen. Whatever it appears to pick up during a conversation is happening in the text sitting in front of it rather than in the model itself, and the GPT-3 paper is fairly blunt about that: "no weight updates are allowed."[^frozen] Which means every conversation starts exactly the same with no aggregated reshaping between sessions.

Now I know that the obvious objection to that are memory systems, so I won't handwave that away, instead let's talk about it. A retrieval layer does help meaningfully, and a model that can look up what it wrote last Tuesday is more useful than one that can't. It falls short of learning, though, in a way that is worth being very precise about. The model reading its notes is the same model it was before it read them, with the notes now sitting in front of it. Retrieval changes the input; learning changes the reader.

### Endogenous Activity

Now let's start with the correction again, and this one genuinely surprised me while I was researching the content for this discussion. Brains don't idle between tasks. The energy cost of whatever you're doing right now is tiny next to the cost of simply being a brain at all. The extra consumption that comes from changing what you're doing runs "often less than 5% of the baseline level of activity."[^raichle] Underneath that baseline is structure rather than noise. One group recorded from the visual cortex of young ferrets as they grew up, comparing what the cortex did while the animal watched natural scenes against what it did with nothing in particular to look at. Over the months from eye opening to maturity, the resting pattern gradually came to match the watching pattern.[^berkes] The resting brain appears to be running a model of the world it expects to see.

A language model between calls does nothing whatsoever. It isn't waiting, or idling, or considering. The architecture runs strictly forward, one word at a time, having dispensed "with recurrence and convolutions entirely," and nothing carries over from one pass to the next except the text itself.[^attention] I should be precise here, because a running system does hold on to the work it already did on the earlier part of the conversation rather than redoing it, so there is state of a kind. It's state derived entirely from the words in front of it, and it evaporates when the session ends.

Agent loops and sub-agents don't close this gap either, and July is the clearest and most recent illustration I've seen so far of this. What looked from the outside like a persistent adversary operating for five days was a very large number of separate invocations, none of which was running while the others were, coordinating through files left on a shared package proxy. The persistence lived in the filesystem. That's a bureaucracy rather than a mind, and the distinction is practical, not philosophical, because you can go and read a filesystem afterward to find out exactly what happened.

### Intrinsic Stakes

The correction here is that a living thing's goals don't come from anywhere outside the living thing. A body has to hold a narrow range of temperature and chemistry or it stops working, and most of what an animal wants traces back to that requirement one way or another. Damasio and Carvalho argue that feelings are built directly out of it, calling them "a crucial component of the mechanisms of life regulation, from simple to complex."[^damasio] Let's attribute that rather than just assert it, since serious people contest the details, but the narrow point I need from it is uncontroversial: nobody hands an animal its goals.

A model's goals arrive in a prompt and leave with it. There is nothing the system is trying to protect, nothing it loses if the task fails, and no condition it has to maintain in order to keep being what it is.

Two separate claims are sitting underneath that, and they carry very different weight, so let me pull them apart. The first is that a system needs something at stake before it can understand anything, and that one is my own view rather than a settled finding. It's arguable, plenty of people would argue it, and I hold it loosely enough to change my mind if somebody gives me a good reason to. The second is that today's systems have nothing at stake, and that one is a plain description of how they are built rather than a view anybody needs to share with me.

The practical consequence is the part I'd actually put in a governance document. The system never gets tired, bored or resentful, which is genuinely useful. It also never gets uneasy. It will not come to you and say that something about this quarter's numbers looks wrong, because nothing is at stake for it in the numbers being right, and nobody asked.

### Grounding

Stevan Harnad named the symbol grounding problem back in 1990, and the way I hold onto it is this. Picture trying to learn a language you don't speak from a dictionary written entirely in that same language. Every word is defined using other words, each of those is defined using still others, and nothing anywhere in the book ever touches the world outside it. That's the problem, and the question it asks is how the symbols a system handles ever come to be about anything at all.[^harnad]

Harnad came back to this for language models in 2025, and he has no patience for the usual defense, which is that these systems are grounded indirectly through all the grounded human writing they trained on. His answer to that: "your own explanation is not 'indirectly' grounded: it is not grounded at all!" He's fairer than the flat skeptic position usually gets credit for, though, and I'd want his concession quoted alongside it. "It is not true that it understands. But it is also not true that we understand how it can do what it can do."

Here's where part one's phrase earns its keep. For a deployed system, its tools are its body. The integrations you grant are its senses and its limbs, and its world is exactly what those tools can perceive and touch, no larger and no smaller. A model with a read-only connection to a ticketing system lives in a world made of tickets. Grant it the ability to send mail and you've enlarged its world, not just its permissions.

That comparison has a limit worth naming, and this next part is my reading rather than anyone's finding. A body is in one place at a time, and the same senses that let an animal act are the ones that let it notice. A system running in a dozen places at once, with tools that mostly go off and do things without reporting back, has a world shaped quite differently from any animal's. I think the shape of that world, rather than the size of the model sitting inside it, decides most of what the system can and cannot notice.

## What This Doesn't Settle

I've defined comprehension by absence, which means I've described a hole rather than a shape. That's deliberate, and it has limits I should be honest about.

Does the kind of internal structure that the interpretability work keeps turning up amount to understanding? I honestly don't know, and I don't believe anybody else does yet either. The people who have thought hardest about it disagree with each other in public, which is usually a sign that a question is still open rather than a sign that one side is being obtuse. The popular error is treating it as settled in the yes direction, because the output reads so well. The dismissal camp's error is treating it as settled in the no direction, because the training recipe is so simple. Both are the fluency error wearing different clothes, one trusting the output and one trusting the recipe.

What I'd defend is narrower. The four properties above are checkable, and they come out the same way regardless of how the understanding question resolves. A system with better circuits inside it still has frozen weights, still does nothing between calls, still has nothing at stake, and still lives in a world bounded by whatever you connected it to. Those are facts about how the thing was built and where you plugged it in, and those are the facts a governance decision actually rests on.

## What Buyers Want to Hear

Part one looked at what operators are tempted to deploy. This is the other side of the same market: what buyers are tempted to believe.

Nobody writes a business case for a system that predicts plausible continuations of text. Plenty of people will sign one for a system that understands your customers. So the word shows up in the pitch, along with reasoning and judgment and intent, and none of it is exactly false, because none of those words has an agreed technical meaning here. They're doing commercial work rather than descriptive work.

I couldn't find a source for this next part that I'd be comfortable citing, so I'll just say it plainly and you can judge it on its merits. The market rewards the fluency error, and it does so at precisely the point where a buyer is least equipped to test the claim. A demo is an output-quality test. It's the same test that convinced people about ELIZA in 1966, run at higher resolution and with a much larger budget behind it.

The tell is easy enough to check, and I'd check it early. Ask what happens to the system's answer when somebody updates the underlying policy document and nobody rebuilds the search index behind it. If "understands your customers" means anything you can operate, it means there's a path from that document to the answer, a guarantee about how fresh it stays, and somebody whose actual job is maintaining both. If the answer to that question is a shrug, then the word understanding was doing work that plumbing should have been doing.

## Three More Rules, the Chaff, and the Ordinary Middle

Part one ended with three rules about objectives and tools. Three more follow from the four properties, and they're for the same person: whoever decides what one of these systems is for and what it's allowed to touch.

**Rule 4: interrogate the plumbing, not the model.** Which model a vendor uses is close to the least interesting question you can ask, and it's the one that gets asked first. The interesting questions are where the body of documents it searches actually comes from, how often that gets refreshed, whose job it is when the contents go stale, and what the system does when it searches and finds nothing useful. "Understanding your customers" lives in those four answers, entirely.

**Rule 5: monitoring is the substitute for the conscience the system doesn't have.** A system with nothing at stake will not notice its own drift, will not flag that a policy changed underneath it, and will not tell you that the last two hundred cases it handled were subtly wrong in the same direction. A better model changes none of that, because the thing that would produce the warning is exactly the thing it doesn't have. The uneasiness a person in that seat would have felt has to be replaced by something you deliberately build and somebody actually watches.

**Rule 6: when the system's tools change, its world changes.** Adding an integration is not a feature request. It redraws the boundary of what the system can see and what it can touch, and everything you decided about the system was decided inside the old boundary. Your risk assessment changes, and if part one's argument holds, so does the shape of the objective. Work through all three again together, and put that review somewhere your change-approval process will actually trip over it.

Notice what isn't on that list. AGI timelines don't appear, because no rule above changes if the date moves. Benchmark leaderboards don't appear, because none of the four properties is on any leaderboard. "Reasoning" as a product word doesn't appear, because it's a marketing category rather than a capability claim you can test. Headline job-loss forecasts don't appear, because they're forecasts. That's the chaff, and I find it takes up most of the airtime while changing none of the decisions.

What's left is the ordinary middle. The real failure modes here are Goodhart's law, automation bias, and accountability gaps: a metric that stops measuring what you meant, a person who defers to the machine because the machine is usually right, and a decision nobody can later be shown to have made. None of that is new. Researchers reviewing two decades of work on how people use automation found all three well documented long before this generation of technology existed.[^automation] I'd read that literature with one caveat, which is that its framing tempts you into blaming the operator when the finding is really about how the system was designed around them.

So both extremes are making the same mistake from opposite ends. The doom picture and the dismissal picture each assume the model is the whole story, and the model is the part we understand least and steer least directly. Everything we can inspect and change sits around it: the objective, the tools, where its information comes from, the monitoring, and the person accountable for each of those. That's grounds for measured confidence rather than either fear or complacency, and it comes with a bill attached, because doing it properly costs real money and real headcount and produces no demo at the end.

Which brings me to the question I'd put to anyone currently evaluating one of these systems. Set aside what the model can do, since you'll find out soon enough. Who in your organization would be the first to notice if it started being confidently wrong in the same direction every time, and what exactly would they be looking at when they noticed?

[^damasio]: Antonio Damasio and Gil B.
