### C-21
- **Status:** FIT (abstract-only)
- **Citation:** Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87–114. (BBS target article with open peer commentary.)
- **URL/DOI:** doi:10.1017/S0140525X01003922 — PMID 11515286
- **Claim needed:** Working memory capacity is roughly four chunks.
- **Supporting passage:** "Others have since suggested that there is a more precise capacity limit, but that it is only three to five chunks." (published abstract; abstract-only access)
  - Boundary conditions, same abstract: "rehearsal and long-term memory cannot be used to combine stimulus items into chunks of an unknown size; nor can storage mechanisms that are not capacity-limited, such as sensory memory, allow the capacity-limited storage mechanism to be refilled during recall."
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:**
  - Cowan does not claim a universal "4". He claims a *narrow range*, **three to five chunks**. The title's "4" is the midpoint, and — like Miller's 7 — partly a rhetorical device.
  - The limit is only observable under four specified conditions, which the abstract enumerates: (1) information overload forcing chunks down to single stimulus items, (2) steps taken to block recoding of items into larger chunks, (3) performance discontinuities caused by the limit, and (4) indirect effects of the limit.
  - Cowan explicitly distinguishes **"pure" capacity estimates** (rehearsal, chunking and long-term-memory support all blocked) from **"compound" STM limits** where the number of separately held chunks is unknown. Everyday performance — phone numbers, chess positions, a skilled reader's span — is compound, and is routinely higher than four.
  - Consequence for the post: "you can hold about four things in mind" is fair only with the rider *when you are prevented from rehearsing them or grouping them into larger units*. Without that rider, a cognitive psychologist will object, correctly.
- **Field status in 2026:** Qualified, not overturned. The ~4-chunk figure is still the standard reference point and Cowan has maintained it across later work, but two live disputes qualify it. (a) **Slots vs. resources:** discrete fixed-slot models are contested by continuous-resource models, in which apparent "guessing" beyond 3–4 items is what low per-item precision looks like rather than an empty slot; computational-modelling work through 2024–2026 has not settled it. (b) **What you count:** Morra, Patella & Muscella (2024) revive Pascual-Leone's objection that Cowan's 4 counts only declarative chunks and undercounts attentional resources devoted to procedural units — count those and the estimate drifts toward seven (*Journal of Cognition*, doi:10.5334/joc.387).
- **Notes / risks:** Safe to cite; the risk is over-claiming precision. Recommended framing: "roughly three to five chunks once you strip out rehearsal and chunking." Avoid "the brain has four slots" — that is the contested reading, not the safe one.

### C-22
- **Status:** FIT (abstract-only) — with a scope caveat that matters
- **Citation:** Loftus, E. F., & Palmer, J. C. (1974). Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 13(5), 585–589.
- **URL/DOI:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022537174800113 (Elsevier PII S0022537174800113; the corresponding DOI is 10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3 — derived from the PII, not independently confirmed, so prefer the URL or the plain citation if you want zero risk)
- **Claim needed:** Memory is reconstructive rather than a recording.
- **Supporting passage:** "These results are consistent with the view that the questions asked subsequent to an event can cause a reconstruction in one's memory of that event." (published abstract, final sentence; abstract-only access)
  - Method sentence, same abstract: "The question, 'About how fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?' elicited higher estimates of speed than questions which used the verbs collided, bumped, contacted, or hit in place of smashed."
- **Why this one, over Bartlett and Schacter:**
  - **Bartlett (1932), *Remembering*** — the origin of the reconstructive view, but the worst pick here. It is a book, hard to point a lay reader at, and its method is loose by modern standards: instructions to participants were ambiguous about whether they were to recall accurately or merely retell, and Gauld & Stephenson (1967) reportedly reproduced Bartlett's escalating distortions *only* when participants were not told to aim for accuracy. Wheeler & Roediger likewise found little distortion using the same material under accuracy instructions. Bartlett is a landmark, not evidence.
  - **Schacter (1999), "The seven sins of memory"** (*American Psychologist*, 54(3), 182–203, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.54.3.182) — authoritative and readable, and it explicitly frames the seven sins as revealing "the fundamentally constructive nature of human memory." But it is a **review**, i.e. a synthesis of others' primary work. Excellent as a secondary pointer for readers who want the whole picture; not the primary citation the brief asked for.
  - **Loftus & Palmer (1974)** — a primary experimental report, four pages, freely mirrored, instantly intelligible to a lay reader ("smashed" vs. "hit" changed what people remembered seeing), and its own abstract uses the word *reconstruction*. Best fit for the slot.
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:**
  - The study demonstrates that **post-event language alters what is later reported as remembered**. It does not, on its own, establish the broader thesis that *all* remembering is reconstruction. Do not let the post's sentence run further than the study does — pair the general claim with Schacter (1999) and cite Loftus & Palmer as the demonstration.
  - Laboratory conditions: staged films of traffic accidents, US college-student participants, small samples (Experiment 1: 45 students — the Experiment 2 sample size I could not verify without full text). Ecological validity to real eyewitnesses was contested from the start.
  - The broken-glass result is a *change in reported memory*; whether the underlying trace was overwritten, blocked, or merely reported differently under demand characteristics was left open in 1974 and is still argued.
- **Field status in 2026:** Solid. This is one of the classic memory findings that came through the replication crisis in good shape. The misinformation effect has been replicated widely, including contemporary registered replications and extensions (e.g. a 2025 replication/extension of Loftus et al. 1978, PMID 41021526; O'Donnell et al. 2025 registered-report replication of Loftus 1979, doi:10.1111/lcrp.12242). Loftus's own 2026 retrospective, "The history of an idea: the misinformation effect" (*Legal and Criminological Psychology*, doi:10.1111/lcrp.70020), reviews the record.
- **Notes / risks:** The honest caveats are (a) modern work finds the effect partly driven by demand characteristics, and (b) not every Loftus-lineage finding replicated — the "inoculation" effect notably did not. Neither touches the core claim. The one thing to avoid is implying the 1974 study proves memories are *erased and rewritten*; it shows reported memory is shaped by later input, which is what the post needs anyway.

### C-23
- **Status:** FIT (abstract-only) for Diekelmann & Born — but see the recommendation to cite Klinzing et al. (2019) alongside or instead
- **Citation:** Diekelmann, S., & Born, J. (2010). The memory function of sleep. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 11(2), 114–126.
- **URL/DOI:** doi:10.1038/nrn2762 — PMID 20046194
- **Claim needed:** Sleep consolidation compresses/restructures experience into long-term structure — the asymmetry against frozen model weights.
- **Supporting passage:** "Consolidation during sleep promotes both quantitative and qualitative changes of memory representations." (published abstract; abstract-only access)
  - Mechanism sentence, same abstract: "During SWS, slow oscillations, spindles and ripples — at minimum cholinergic activity — coordinate the re-activation and redistribution of hippocampus-dependent memories to neocortical sites."
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:**
  - **(a) Hedging on mechanism vs. evidence.** The abstract's REM half is explicitly conditional: local increases in plasticity-related immediate-early gene activity "**might favour** the subsequent synaptic consolidation of memories in the cortex." The SWS/system-consolidation half is stated more confidently than the REM/synaptic-consolidation half, and the post should not flatten the two into one settled story.
  - The whole effect is conditional on circumstances — the abstract's own opening: sleep optimizes consolidation "depending on the specific conditions of learning and the timing of sleep." Sleep is not a general-purpose memory improver.
  - The clean SWS = systems consolidation / REM = synaptic consolidation division is a framework the authors propose, not an established partition; it competes with the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis (Tononi & Cirelli), which the 2010 review addresses rather than settles.
- **Field status in 2026:** Qualified — and the qualification is significant enough that the post's framing needs care.
  - **Prefer or add Klinzing, J. G., Niethard, N., & Born, J. (2019). Mechanisms of systems memory consolidation during sleep. *Nature Neuroscience*, 22(10), 1598–1610, doi:10.1038/s41593-019-0467-3 (PMID 31451802).** It is nine years newer, from the same lab, and its abstract states the load-bearing sentence more directly than the 2010 paper does: "Repeated neuronal replay of representations originating from the hippocampus during slow-wave sleep leads to a **gradual transformation and integration of representations in neocortical networks**." That is precisely the "compression into long-term structure" the post needs. It also frames consolidation as "an active systems consolidation process that is embedded in a process of global synaptic downscaling," which absorbs the Tononi/Cirelli position rather than opposing it.
  - **The honesty caveat the post should carry:** Cordi & Rasch (2021), "How robust are sleep-mediated memory benefits?", *Current Opinion in Neurobiology*, 67, 1–7 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959438820300994). Several headline findings in this literature failed to replicate or replicated only under narrow conditions; the authors conclude the effects of sleep on memory are "smaller, more task-dependent, less SWS-related, less robust and less long-lasting" than the field assumed, and call for better reproducibility standards.
- **Notes / risks:** The post makes consolidation the load-bearing asymmetry between brains and frozen weights. That asymmetry is safe at the qualitative level — brains reorganize representations offline, model weights do not change between training runs. It is *not* safe at the quantitative level: do not cite specific percentage memory gains from sleep, and do not present the SWS/REM division of labour as established. Cite 2019 as the mechanism, 2010 as the classic review, and acknowledge Cordi & Rasch in a clause so a sleep researcher reading the post sees you knew.

### C-25
- **Status:** FOUND — the paper is real and the citation is right, but **the claim as worded is the popular version, not Raichle's**
- **Citation:** Raichle, M. E., MacLeod, A. M., Snyder, A. Z., Powers, W. J., Gusnard, D. A., & Shulman, G. L. (2001). A default mode of brain function. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*, 98(2), 676–682.
- **URL/DOI:** doi:10.1073/pnas.98.2.676 — PMCID PMC14647
- **Claim needed:** The default mode network is highly active at rest, meaning brains do not idle.
- **Supporting passage:** "A baseline or control state is fundamental to the understanding of most complex systems. Defining a baseline state in the human brain, arguably our most complex system, poses a particular challenge. Many suspect that left unconstrained, its activity will vary unpredictably. Despite this prediction we identify a baseline state of the normal adult human brain in terms of the brain oxygen extraction fraction or OEF." (published abstract, opening; abstract-only access)
- **How Raichle actually framed it — be precise here:**
  - The paper is about **defining a physiological baseline**, not about measuring how "busy" the resting brain is. The baseline is defined by the **oxygen extraction fraction (OEF)** — oxygen used divided by oxygen delivered — which the paper reports as "remarkably uniform in the awake but resting state (e.g., lying quietly with eyes closed)."
  - The default mode network was identified **by subtraction, from task-induced *decreases*.** A set of midline regions — posterior cingulate/precuneus and medial prefrontal cortex — consistently *deactivate* below this baseline across a wide range of goal-directed tasks. Raichle's inference is that these areas are *tonically active in the baseline state* and that functions specific to that state are temporarily suspended when the task begins.
  - So the accurate sentence is not "the DMN lights up at rest." It is: **there is an organized, reproducible baseline state; a specific set of regions is tonically active within it; and task engagement turns those regions *down*, not up.** "Highly active at rest" is a press-coverage compression, and a neuroscientist reader will notice.
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:**
  - "Rest" here is an operationalization — lying quietly, eyes closed — not an absence of mental activity. The 2001 paper does not characterize what the baseline state is *for*; it identifies that one exists and is stable.
  - The energy claim that actually carries "brains do not idle" is not in this paper.
- **Field status in 2026:** The existence of the DMN and of a high-cost intrinsic baseline is settled and is one of the most replicated findings in human neuroimaging. What the DMN *does* (self-referential thought, mind-wandering, prospection, semantic/schema processing, or a mixture) remains actively debated, as does whether "resting state" is a meaningful control condition at all.
- **Notes / risks:** **Recommended fix:** keep Raichle et al. (2001) as the citation for the default mode / baseline, and add **Raichle, M. E., & Mintun, M. A. (2006). Brain work and brain imaging. *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, 29, 449–476, doi:10.1146/annurev.neuro.29.051605.112819** for the "brains do not idle" point specifically — that is the source for the widely quoted figure that the *additional* energy consumed by task-evoked activity is small, often **under 5%** of ongoing baseline consumption. For a modern one-stop review, Raichle's "The brain's default mode network," *Annual Review of Neuroscience* (2015), doi:10.1146/annurev-neuro-071013-014030. Do **not** cite the *Scientific American* "The Brain's Dark Energy" piece — it is press coverage of exactly this material and the batch rules exclude it.

### C-26
- **Status:** FOUND — Clark supports "the brain is a prediction machine"; the abstract does **not** carry the "even without task input" clause
- **Citation:** Clark, A. (2013). Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 36(3), 181–204. (Target article with open peer commentary and author's response.)
- **URL/DOI:** doi:10.1017/S0140525X12000477 — PMID 23663408
- **Claim needed:** The cortex continuously generates predictions even without task input.
- **Supporting passage:** "Brains, it has recently been argued, are essentially prediction machines. They are bundles of cells that support perception and action by constantly attempting to match incoming sensory inputs with top-down expectations or predictions. This is achieved using a hierarchical generative model that aims to minimize prediction error within a bidirectional cascade of cortical processing." (published abstract; abstract-only access)
- **Where the claim over-reaches:** every verified sentence describes prediction *against incoming sensory input*. "Continuously generates predictions **even without task input**" is a stronger, different claim — about spontaneous activity — and I could not confirm it from the abstract. Two options:
  1. **Soften** the post's sentence to "the cortex is continuously predicting, not passively receiving" — fully covered by the quote above.
  2. **Keep the strong version and add the empirical source for it:** Berkes, P., Orbán, G., Lengyel, M., & Fiser, J. (2011). Spontaneous cortical activity reveals hallmarks of an optimal internal model of the environment. *Science*, 331(6013), 83–87, doi:10.1126/science.1195870 (PMC3065813). Its abstract states the prediction that spontaneous and evoked activity "should match if the model is statistically optimal," and reports in awake ferrets that "similarity between spontaneous and evoked activities increased with age and was specific to responses evoked by natural scenes." That is the actual evidence that cortex runs its generative model in the absence of driving input.
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:**
  - This is a **BBS target article**: it was published *with* about thirty peer commentaries and Clark's response, precisely because it is a programmatic proposal rather than a result. Saying so in the post is not a weakness; it is what makes the citation honest.
  - Clark himself presents predictive processing as a framework that *may* unify perception, action and attention — the abstract's register is "offers a unifying model," not "has been shown."
- **Field status in 2026:** **Contested — do not present as settled.** Predictive processing is influential and productive, but it has serious critics on two fronts.
  - *Empirical:* Walsh, McGovern, Clark & O'Connell (2020), "Evaluating the neurophysiological evidence for predictive processing as a model of perception," *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*, doi:10.1111/nyas.14321 — finds the neurophysiological evidence considerably more equivocal than the framework's popularity suggests.
  - *Methodological/philosophical:* Litwin & Miłkowski (2020), "Unification by fiat: arrested development of predictive processing," *Cognitive Science*, doi:10.1111/cogs.12867 — argues PP's unification is nominal, that PP models are loosely tied to their computational and biological foundations and are "seldom empirically validated." A recurring charge is that the free parameter of *precision* makes the framework accommodate any result (large errors = low precision; small errors = high precision), i.e. hard to falsify.
- **Notes / risks:** Recommended phrasing for the post: "on the influential predictive-processing account (Clark 2013), the cortex is continuously predicting rather than passively receiving — a framework that is widely adopted but still argued over." That is defensible to a hostile reader. "The brain is a prediction machine, full stop" is not.

### C-27
- **Status:** CUT (recommended)
- **Citation:** Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 11(2), 127–138.
- **URL/DOI:** doi:10.1038/nrn2787 — PMID 20068583
- **Claim needed:** (Optional backstop to C-26 — that the brain minimizes prediction error / surprise as a general principle.)
- **Supporting passage:** "one key theme runs through each of these theories — optimization. Furthermore, if we look closely at what is optimized, the same quantity keeps emerging, namely value (expected reward, expected utility) or its complement, surprise (prediction error, expected cost)." (published abstract; abstract-only access)
- **Assessment for a business audience:** Cut it. Friston 2010 adds cost and no clarity for this post. It buys one thing Clark does not — the claim that prediction-error minimization is a single unifying imperative across perception, action and learning — and pays for it with a formalism (variational free energy, Markov blankets, active inference) that no business reader will follow and that the post has no room to explain. Worse, it imports a much harder credibility problem: the free-energy principle is the most contested object in this whole batch. Friston has himself characterized it as a mathematical principle rather than an empirically falsifiable hypothesis, and critics from Litwin & Miłkowski through Bruineberg and colleagues ("The Emperor's New Markov Blankets," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 2022) have pressed the charge that it is unfalsifiable, that it conflates thermodynamic and information-theoretic free energy, and that it can be retrofitted to any observation. Citing it signals depth to roughly the ten readers who already know the literature, and those ten are also the readers most likely to think it weakens the piece. Clark (2013) plus Berkes et al. (2011) carries the argument with less exposure.
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:** n/a if cut. If the author insists on citing it, the post must say it is a proposed unifying principle, not an empirical result, and must not describe it as established neuroscience.
- **Field status in 2026:** Contested, prominently and publicly.
- **Notes / risks:** The only scenario where it earns its place is if the post explicitly argues *why brains must predict* (a survival/thermodynamic argument). It does not appear to need that.

### C-30
- **Status:** FOUND — correct source located; the abstract carries an adjacent claim, not the exact one
- **Citation (recommended primary):** Damasio, A., & Carvalho, G. B. (2013). The nature of feelings: evolutionary and neurobiological origins. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 14(2), 143–152. (Published as an Opinion/Perspective piece — i.e. explicitly a theoretical position, not a research report.)
- **URL/DOI:** doi:10.1038/nrn3403 — PMID 23329161
- **Claim needed:** Biological goals are homeostatic — grounded in the organism's physical constitution and its need to stay alive — rather than externally specified.
- **Supporting passage:** "Feelings are mental experiences of body states. They signify physiological need (for example, hunger), tissue injury (for example, pain), optimal function (for example, well-being), threats to the organism (for example, fear or anger) or specific social interactions (for example, compassion, gratitude or love). **Feelings constitute a crucial component of the mechanisms of life regulation, from simple to complex.**" (published abstract; abstract-only access)
- **Why this over the book — and the one caveat:**
  - **Damasio, A. (2018). *The Strange Order of Things: Life, Feeling, and the Making of Cultures*. New York: Pantheon. ISBN 978-0-307-90875-9 (hardcover, ISBN-10 0307908755).** This is the fullest statement of exactly the claim the post wants — homeostasis as the source of biological value, running continuously from single cells to cultures. But it is a trade book: no DOI, no abstract, and I could not open it to verify a passage, so **I am not supplying a quotation from it.** Cite it as further reading for lay readers; do not attribute a sentence to it that nobody in this session has read.
  - The 2013 paper's abstract gets you *feelings as instruments of life regulation*. It does not, in the abstract, say *goals are homeostatic*. That step is made in the body text, which I could not reach. Hence FOUND, not FIT.
  - **Worth considering instead, and possibly the best fit of all three for this particular post:** Man, K., & Damasio, A. (2019). Homeostasis and soft robotics in the design of feeling machines. *Nature Machine Intelligence*, 1(10), 446–452, doi:10.1038/s42256-019-0103-7. It is peer-reviewed, has a DOI, and is Damasio arguing *directly about machines* — that a machine would need homeostatic vulnerability, not just an objective function, to have anything like feelings. For a post contrasting biological goals with model objectives, that is the citation that does the most work per word. (Its published abstract is a single sentence and does not carry the full claim either, so the same verification caveat applies.)
  - Also relevant for a business audience: Damasio, A., & Damasio, H. (2016), "Exploring the concept of homeostasis and considering its implications for economics," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S016726811500325X).
- **Author-stated limitations the post must repeat:**
  - This is **a theoretical position within affective neuroscience, not a consensus empirical finding** — the 2013 article was published in NRN's Opinion format, which is the journal's label for exactly that. The post should attribute it ("on Damasio's account…"), not state it as fact.
  - The specific mechanistic proposals in the 2013 paper (feelings arising from body states via non-synaptic as well as synaptic signalling, with substrates "at all levels of the nervous system, from individual neurons to subcortical nuclei and cortical regions") are proposals, not established findings.
- **Field status in 2026:** Contested. Damasio's homeostatic/embodied account is one of three or four major live frameworks in affective neuroscience, and the disagreements are foundational rather than cosmetic:
  - **Higher-order accounts (LeDoux and colleagues)** place conscious emotional feeling in cortical, particularly prefrontal, processes and reject the idea that subcortical bodily states constitute feelings.
  - **The theory of constructed emotion (Barrett and colleagues)** agrees with Damasio that the brain's primary job is bodily regulation — but frames it as *allostasis* plus categorization, and denies the biologically basic emotion categories that the Damasio lineage is usually read as endorsing.
  - What *is* broadly agreed across all three camps: bodily regulation (homeostasis/allostasis) is central to what nervous systems are for. That much the post can assert. The disputed part is what feelings *are* and where they live.
- **Notes / risks:** The safe formulation is: *"Damasio argues that biological goals are not assigned from outside but arise from the organism's need to keep itself alive within viable bounds — a theoretical position, though one whose core premise (that nervous systems exist primarily for bodily regulation) is shared even by his critics."* That framing is bulletproof against an affective-neuroscience reader. Asserting the strong version flatly is not.
