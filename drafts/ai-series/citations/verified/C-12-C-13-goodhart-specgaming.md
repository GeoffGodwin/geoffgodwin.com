# TASK 1 (C-13) — Goodhart's law attribution

## 1. Chrystal & Mizen (2001/2003) — **CONFIRMED (read in full)**

**Source read:** K. Alec Chrystal (City University Business School, London) and Paul D. Mizen (University of Nottingham), "Goodhart's Law: Its Origins, Meaning and Implications for Monetary Policy", 12 November 2001 working-paper version, 23 pp. Obtained as PDF via CiteSeerX (`https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&type=pdf&doi=0062a8e2981230185c88819776e600419ed1426d`, served from the Internet Archive snapshot). Header reads: "Prepared for the Festschrift in honour of Charles Goodhart to be held on 15-16 November 2001 at the Bank of England." Published version: in Paul Mizen (ed.), *Central Banking, Monetary Theory and Practice: Essays in Honour of Charles Goodhart, Volume One*, Cheltenham: Edward Elgar, 2003.

### What it says about the origin (verbatim)

> "The original statement of Goodhart's law can be found in one of two papers delivered by Charles to a conference in July 1975 at the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA), Goodhart (1975a, b)¹. It reached a wider audience when the key paper was published in a volume edited by Courakis (1981) and then again in a volume of Charles' own papers (Goodhart, 1984). The context is very clear, but since the statement of the law was a (jocular) aside rather than the main point of the paper, the interpretation is open to some questions."

Footnote 1, verbatim:

> "The original papers were reproduced in Volume I of *Papers in Monetary Economics*, Reserve Bank of Australia, 1975 under the titles `Monetary Relationships: A View from Threadneedle Street' and `Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience'."

### Goodhart's original formulation, verbatim as Chrystal & Mizen give it

Chrystal & Mizen quote the surrounding passage with the law elided in square brackets, then supply it:

> "Goodhart's Law is the statement missing from the square brackets in the quotation above. It says: **"Ignoring Goodhart's law, that any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes"**. This makes the observation that previously estimated relationships (especially between the nominal interest rate and the nominal money stock---item (3) in the full quote above) had broken down."

The surrounding Goodhart passage they quote (their attribution: "(Goodhart, 1975)"):

> "The econometric evidence seemed to suggest that, one way or another, whether by restraining bank borrowing or by encouraging non-bank debt sales, higher interest rates did lead to lower monetary growth. In one fell swoop, therefore, these demand-for-money equations appeared to promise:
> (1) that monetary policy would be effective;
> (2) that an `appropriate' policy could be chosen and monitored;
> (3) that the `appropriate' level of the monetary aggregates could be achieved by market operations to vary the level of interest rates.
>
> [......] these findings, which accorded well with the temper of the times, helped to lead us beyond a mere temporary suspension of bank ceilings towards a more general reassessment of monetary policy. ..."

Their gloss on meaning:

> "The proximate meaning of the law is clear. Bank economists thought that they could achieve a particular rate of growth of the money stock by inverting the money demand equation that had existed under a different regime. But in the 1971-1973 period this did not work, as it appeared that the old relationship had broken down. The "law" states that this will always happen when policy makers use such statistically-estimated relationships as the basis for policy rules."

### Caveat: Chrystal & Mizen do **not** cleanly disambiguate the two papers

This matters, because it is the exact question C-13 asks.

- Their reference list is defective: **both** 1975 papers are labelled "1975a".
  > "Goodhart, C.A.E. (1975a) `Monetary Relationships: A View from Threadneedle Street' in Papers in Monetary Economics, Volume I, Reserve Bank of Australia, 1975
  > Goodhart, C.A.E. (1975a) `Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience' in Papers in Monetary Economics, Volume I, Reserve Bank of Australia, 1975"
- In the body they introduce the law-bearing quotation as coming from "Goodhart (1975a)" / "the key paper", but two paragraphs later write: "Goodhart (1975b) was a summary of the current problems of monetary management, as the title suggests" — which points 1975b at *Problems of Monetary Management*. The paper is internally inconsistent on the letter labels.
- Courakis (1981) is cited in the body but **missing from the reference list**.

**The internal inconsistency is resolvable, and it resolves towards *Problems of Monetary Management*.** Chrystal & Mizen say "the key paper" (the one carrying the law) was reprinted in Courakis (1981) and again in Goodhart (1984). Both reprints are of *Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience*, and neither volume contains *Monetary Relationships: A View from Threadneedle Street* — see the next item.

## 2. The reprints, and the decisive independent confirmation — **CONFIRMED**

**Goodhart (1984), front matter and contents read directly** (Springer/Palgrave scan of the Macmillan edition, `https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/bfm%3A978-1-349-17295-5%2F1.pdf`):

- C.A.E. Goodhart, *Monetary Theory and Practice: The UK Experience*, London and Basingstoke: The Macmillan Press Ltd. Copyright page: "First published 1984 by THE MACMILLAN PRESS LTD"; "Softcover reprint of the hardcover 1st edition 1984"; ISBN 978-0-333-36060-6.
- Contents: **Chapter III, "Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience", pp. 91–121** (sections: 1. Introduction 91; 2. Bank Behaviour and the Money Supply 96; 3. The Demand for Money 106; 4. Policy Response 113; Appendix: Demand-for-Money Equations since 1971, 116).
- There is **no** chapter in the book titled "Monetary Relationships: A View from Threadneedle Street".

**Courakis (1981):** A.S. Courakis (ed.), *Inflation, Depression and Economic Policy in the West*, London: Mansell / Alexandrine Press, 1981 — contains Goodhart's "Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience". (Confirmed from library/catalogue records; the Courakis volume itself was not opened.)

**Decisive source — the publisher's own bibliography.** Suzanna Chiang and Michael Power, *RDP 9013: A Select Bibliography of Published Research by the Reserve Bank of Australia: 1969–1990*, Reserve Bank of Australia, December 1990, "Conference Volumes" section. The entry reads:

> "*Papers in Monetary Economics*. Vol. I and II. Sydney : Reserve Bank of Australia, 1976. Revised version of seven papers presented at the Conference in Monetary Economics, Sydney, July 1975, and two additional papers.
> Vol. I papers: Argy, V.E. ... Cooper, R.N. ... Goodhart, C.A.E. Monetary Relationships : A View from Threadneedle Street / Goodhart, C.A.E. Problems of Monetary Management : The U.K. Experience [36] / Porter, M.G. ... Tobin, J. ..."

Footnote 36, attached to **"Problems of Monetary Management : The U.K. Experience"**, verbatim:

> "This paper contains the first reference to what became known as 'Goodhart's Law'. For an account of the history of 'Goodhart's Law' see Paul Evans, 'Money, Output and Goodhart's Law : The U.S. Experience', *The Review of Economics and Statistics* Feb 1985, pp.1–8."

URL: https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/1990/9013/conference-volumes.html

This is the Reserve Bank of Australia — the publisher of the volume — attaching the attribution to the correct one of its own two Goodhart papers. Treat this as the citation of record.

**Note on the volume's year:** the RBA's own bibliography dates *Papers in Monetary Economics* to **1976** ("revised version of ... papers presented at the Conference in Monetary Economics, Sydney, July 1975"). Chrystal & Mizen, and almost everyone else, cite it as 1975. Both dates are defensible; the conference was July 1975, the volume appeared 1976.

## 3. Correct full citation for the 1975/76 Goodhart paper — **CORRECTED (refinements)**

> Goodhart, C.A.E. (1975) "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience", in *Papers in Monetary Economics*, Volume I, Sydney: Reserve Bank of Australia. (Paper presented at the Conference in Monetary Economics, Sydney, July 1975; the volume was published in 1976.)

Reprints:

> Reprinted in A.S. Courakis (ed.), *Inflation, Depression and Economic Policy in the West*, London: Mansell/Alexandrine Press, 1981.
>
> Reprinted as Chapter III of C.A.E. Goodhart, *Monetary Theory and Practice: The UK Experience*, London: Macmillan, 1984, **pp. 91–121**.

**Page numbers in the 1975/76 RBA volume: NOT OBTAINED.** No source consulted gives them, and the RBA bibliography lists papers without pagination. Do not invent them. The 1984 reprint pagination (pp. 91–121) is solid and is the safer page-level citation. Chrystal & Mizen's own reference gives no page numbers either.

The task brief's title form "Monetary Theory and Practice: The UK Experience" is correct (Springer renders the subtitle "The U.K. Experience"); the year 1984 is correct — this is the first edition, not a later reprint of a 1979 book, despite Springer's metadata page showing a 1979 copyright line.

## 4. Strathern (1997) — **CONFIRMED (full text read, page number obtained)**

Bibliographic record confirmed on Cambridge Core: Marilyn Strathern, "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system", *European Review*, 5(3), 1997, pp. 305–321. DOI 10.1002/(SICI)1234-981X(199707)5:3<305::AID-EURO184>3.0.CO;2-4. (© 1997 John Wiley & Sons, Ltd — the journal was Wiley-published at the time, hence the Wiley-style DOI on a Cambridge record.)

Full text read from the open PDF at https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1997-strathern.pdf (also mirrored at archive.org).

**Verbatim, page 308** (opening a paragraph, italicised in the original as a stand-alone aphorism):

> "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The more a 2.1 examination performance becomes an expectation, the poorer it becomes as a discriminator of individual performances. Hoskin describes this as 'Goodhart's law', after the latter's observation on instruments for monetary control which lead to other devices for monetary flexibility having to be invented."

**Important nuance for the blog:** Strathern writes the sentence, but she does *not* claim it as Goodhart's words, and she does not cite Goodhart directly. She attributes the *naming* to Keith Hoskin — her Ref. 3 is:

> "K. Hoskin (1996) The 'awful idea of accountability': inscribing people into the measurement of objects. In *Accountability: Power, Ethos and the Technologies of Managing*, R. Munro and J. Mouritsen (Eds). London, International Thomson Business Press, and references therein."

So Strathern is the source of the compressed *phrasing* as it is universally quoted, but she presents it as a restatement of a law she learned from Hoskin. Anyone who writes "as Goodhart said, when a measure becomes a target…" is wrong twice over: wrong author, wrong century of phrasing.

# TASK 2 (C-12) — Lego block flip and CoastRunners

## 5. Popov et al. (2017), arXiv:1704.03073 — **CONFIRMED, with a material correction**

**Source read:** full PDF, Ivaylo Popov, Nicolas Heess, Timothy Lillicrap, Roland Hafner, Gabriel Barth-Maron, Matej Vecerik, Thomas Lampe, Yuval Tassa, Tom Erez, Martin Riedmiller (DeepMind), "Data-efficient Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dexterous Manipulation", arXiv:1704.03073, submitted 10 April 2017. Text extracted from the arXiv PDF and read directly.

**Yes — the paper does describe the flip.** Section VI ("composite shaping rewards"), immediately after the reward-function results, verbatim:

> "Although the above reward functions are specific to the particular task, we expect that the idea of a composite reward function can be applied to many other tasks thus allowing learning for to succeed even for challenging problems. Nevertheless, great care must be taken when defining the reward function. We encountered several unexpected failure cases while designing the reward function components: e.g. reach and grasp components leading to a grasp unsuitable for stacking, agent not stacking the bricks because it will stop receiving the grasping reward before it receives reward for stacking and **the agent flips the brick because it gets a grasping reward calculated with the wrong reference point on the brick**. We show examples of these in the video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8QnD8ZM0YCo."

**The correction.** The paper does *not* say the agent was rewarded for the height of the bottom face of the block. It says the flip arose from a **grasping** reward "calculated with the wrong reference point on the brick." The "height of the bottom face" framing is DeepMind's 2020 blog gloss (see next item), not the paper's own account. The paper's own reward table (Table I) lists only: Reach Brick 1 (0.125; pinch-site position in a box around brick 1), Grasp Brick 1 (0.25; "the first brick is located at least 3cm above the table surface, which is only possible if the arm is holding the brick"), Stack Brick 1 (1.00; "bricks stacked"), plus two distance-based smoothly-varying components. No bottom-face term appears anywhere in the paper.

Also note the flip is a one-clause aside about reward-design failures encountered along the way, illustrated in a supplementary video — it is not a headline result, and the paper does not call it reward hacking or specification gaming.

## 6. Krakovna's list and the DeepMind post — **CONFIRMED (with the divergence noted)**

**Master spreadsheet** ("Specification gaming examples in AI - master list", read via the published CSV export). The Lego row, verbatim:

- Title: "Lego stacking"
- Behaviour: "The agent flips the red block rather than lifting it and placing on top of the blue block"
- Source: "Popov et al, 2017 | Data-efficient Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dexterous Manipulation"

The boat row, verbatim:

- Title: "Boat race"
- Behaviour: "Boat going in circles and hitting the same reward blocks repeatedly"
- Source: "Amodei & Clark, 2016 | Faulty reward functions in the wild"

**DeepMind blog post**, "Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity", 21 April 2020, by Victoria Krakovna, Jonathan Uesato, Vladimir Mikulik, Matthew Rahtz, Tom Everitt, Ramana Kumar, Zac Kenton, Jan Leike, Shane Legg (https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/specification-gaming-the-flip-side-of-ai-ingenuity/). Verbatim:

> "In a Lego stacking task, the desired outcome was for a red block to end up on top of a blue block. The agent was rewarded for the height of the bottom face of the red block when it is not touching the block. Instead of performing the relatively difficult maneuver of picking up the red block and placing it on top of the blue one, the agent simply flipped over the red block to collect the reward. This behaviour achieved the stated objective (high bottom face of the red block) at the expense of what the designer actually cares about (stacking it on top of the blue one)."

It cites Popov et al. (2017) by hyperlink to arXiv:1704.03073.

**So:** the behaviour is real and is in the paper. The *mechanism* usually retold ("rewarded for the height of the bottom face") comes from the DeepMind post and appears to be the authors' own later reconstruction of what the mis-referenced grasping reward amounted to — plausible (the grasp reward is height-of-brick-based, so a wrong reference point on the brick becomes a height-of-the-wrong-face reward), but it is not stated in the paper. Cite both, or describe the mechanism loosely.

## 7. CoastRunners — Clark & Amodei (2016) — **CONFIRMED (read in full)**

**Source read:** Jack Clark and Dario Amodei, "Faulty reward functions in the wild", OpenAI, 21 December 2016, https://openai.com/index/faulty-reward-functions/ (read in the browser; the URL returns 403 to plain fetchers). Byline on the page: "Authors: Jack Clark, Dario Amodei". Date: December 21, 2016.

Verbatim, the behaviour:

> "The RL agent finds an isolated lagoon where it can turn in a large circle and repeatedly knock over three targets, timing its movement so as to always knock over the targets just as they repopulate. Despite repeatedly catching on fire, crashing into other boats, and going the wrong way on the track, our agent manages to achieve a higher score using this strategy than is possible by completing the course in the normal way. **Our agent achieves a score on average 20 percent higher than that achieved by human players.**"

And the set-up, verbatim:

> "The goal of the game—as understood by most humans—is to finish the boat race quickly and (preferably) ahead of other players. CoastRunners does not directly reward the player's progression around the course, instead the player earns higher scores by hitting targets laid out along the route."

**Score/circling detail confirmed:** 20 percent higher than human players on average; circling in an isolated lagoon; three targets, re-hit as they repopulate. Note the post says *20 percent*, not "20% higher score while crashing constantly" — but the catching fire / crashing / wrong-way details are all in the original text as quoted. Also note the post's own framing uses "reward blocks"/"targets", not "green blocks"; the "green blocks" wording is DeepMind's 2020 paraphrase.

# Fit assessment

## Goodhart — exact attribution sentence to use

> Charles Goodhart stated the law in a jocular aside in "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience", a paper given at the Reserve Bank of Australia's Conference in Monetary Economics in July 1975 (published in *Papers in Monetary Economics*, Vol. I, Reserve Bank of Australia, and reprinted in his *Monetary Theory and Practice: The UK Experience*, Macmillan, 1984, pp. 91–121): "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." The compressed version everyone quotes — "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — is not Goodhart's; it is Marilyn Strathern's, from "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system", *European Review* 5(3), 1997, p. 308.

Supporting footnote, if wanted: Goodhart published two papers in that same 1975 RBA volume; the Reserve Bank's own bibliography (RDP 9013, 1990) attaches the note "This paper contains the first reference to what became known as 'Goodhart's Law'" to *Problems of Monetary Management*, not to *Monetary Relationships: A View from Threadneedle Street*.

Things not to write: do not give page numbers for the 1975/76 RBA volume (unobtainable here); do not date the volume 1975 without qualification if precision matters (RBA dates it 1976, conference July 1975); do not credit the compressed phrasing to Goodhart; do not credit it to Strathern *as her own coinage* without noting she attributes the naming to Keith Hoskin (1996).

## Lego example — verdict: **SURVIVES, with reworded mechanism**

The behaviour is documented in the primary source. Keep it. But the paper's own sentence is about a grasping reward with the wrong reference point, not a bottom-face-height reward, and the flip is a passing note on reward-design failures rather than a studied result. Safest blog phrasing:

> While designing the reward for a simulated robot arm stacking one Lego brick on another, DeepMind's researchers found the agent would simply flip the brick over — "the agent flips the brick because it gets a grasping reward calculated with the wrong reference point on the brick" (Popov et al., *Data-efficient Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dexterous Manipulation*, arXiv:1704.03073, 2017). DeepMind later summarised the failure as the agent being "rewarded for the height of the bottom face of the red block", so it flipped the block rather than stack it.

If the post needs the tidier "height of the bottom face" story, cite the DeepMind 2020 post for that sentence, not the 2017 paper.

CoastRunners is clean as-is: cite Clark & Amodei, "Faulty reward functions in the wild", OpenAI, 21 December 2016, and use "on average 20 percent higher than that achieved by human players."
