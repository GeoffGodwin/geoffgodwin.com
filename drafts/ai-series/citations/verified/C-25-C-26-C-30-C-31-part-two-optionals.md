## SLOT C-25 — "brains do not idle"

### Item 1 — Raichle, M.E. & Mintun, M.A., "Brain Work and Brain Imaging", *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 29: 449–476 (2006), DOI 10.1146/annurev.neuro.29.051605.112819 — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**

Bibliographic record confirmed from the publisher's own landing page:

> BRAIN WORK AND BRAIN IMAGING
> Marcus E. Raichle and Mark A. Mintun
> Vol. 29:449-476 (Volume publication date July 2006)
> First published as a Review in Advance on April 20, 2006

Full abstract, verbatim:

> Functional brain imaging with positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance imaging
> has been used extensively to map regional changes in brain activity. The signal used by both
> techniques is based on changes in local circulation and metabolism (brain work). Our
> understanding of the cell biology of these changes has progressed greatly in the past decade.
> New insights have emerged on the role of astrocytes in signal transduction as has an
> appreciation of the unique contribution of aerobic glycolysis to brain energy metabolism.
> Likewise our understanding of the neurophysiologic processes responsible for imaging signals
> has progressed from an assumption that spiking activity (output) of neurons is most relevant
> to one focused on their input. Finally, neuroimaging, with its unique metabolic perspective,
> has alerted us to the ongoing and costly intrinsic activity within brain systems that most
> likely represents the largest fraction of the brain's functional activity.

Keywords listed: activation, astrocytes, baseline, blood flow, glycolysis, intrinsic activity,
oxidative phosphorylation.

**The abstract does not contain any percentage.** The body of this review is behind the Annual
Reviews paywall; Unpaywall and Semantic Scholar both report it CLOSED with no repository copy,
and the only full-text copies findable online are on a pirate mirror, which was not used.
**Therefore the exact sentence carrying the figure could not be read in this source.**

The abstract does, however, independently support the qualitative half of the blog's claim —
"the ongoing and costly intrinsic activity within brain systems ... most likely represents the
largest fraction of the brain's functional activity" is Raichle and Mintun's own summary
sentence and is quotable as such.

### Item 1a — the "<5%" figure, verified in an open-access primary source by the same author — **CONFIRMED**

Read in full: Raichle, M.E., "The restless brain: how intrinsic activity organizes brain
function", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 370(1668): 20140172 (2015),
DOI 10.1098/rstb.2014.0172. Open access, CC-BY, read via the Europe PMC full text (PMC4387513).

> In the average adult human, the brain represents about 2% of the total body weight yet it
> accounts for 20% of all the energy consumed [3,4], 10 times that predicted by its weight alone.

> Relative to this very high rate of ongoing energy consumption in the resting state, the
> additional energy consumption associated with changes in brain activity is remarkably small,
> often less than 5% of the baseline level of activity [5].

Reference [5] in that paper's reference list is, verbatim:

> 5. Raichle ME, Mintun MA. 2006. Brain work and brain imaging. *Annu. Rev. Neurosci.* 29,
> 449–476. (10.1146/annurev.neuro.29.051605.112819)

So Raichle himself attributes the figure to the 2006 review. **Exact percentage: "often less
than 5%". What it is a percentage OF: the baseline level of activity — i.e. the brain's
ongoing energy consumption in the resting state.** It is *not* 5% of the body's energy budget,
and *not* 5% of the brain's 20% share. The quantity being measured is the *additional* energy
consumption associated with task-related changes in brain activity, expressed as a fraction of
resting baseline consumption.

### Item 2 — Raichle, M.E., MacLeod, A.M., Snyder, A.Z., Powers, W.J., Gusnard, D.A. & Shulman, G.L., "A default mode of brain function", *PNAS* 98(2): 676–682 (2001), DOI 10.1073/pnas.98.2.676 — **CORRECTED (previous judgement substantially right, one nuance wrong)**

Read in full, free, at PMC14647. Bibliographic details confirmed exactly as cited (PNAS
2001 Jan 16; 98(2): 676–682; PMID 11209064). Published as an Inaugural Article.

**The previous pass was right that the paper defines a baseline and identifies the default mode
by task-induced *decreases*.** Abstract, verbatim (key passages):

> Despite this prediction we identify a baseline state of the normal adult human brain in terms
> of the brain oxygen extraction fraction or OEF.

> Areas of activation were conspicuous by their absence. All significant deviations from the
> mean hemisphere OEF were increases, signifying deactivations, and resided almost exclusively
> in the visual system.

> Defining the baseline state of an area in this manner attaches meaning to a group of areas
> that consistently exhibit decreases from this baseline, during a wide variety of goal-directed
> behaviors monitored with positron-emission tomography and functional MRI. These decreases
> suggest the existence of an organized, baseline default mode of brain function that is
> suspended during specific goal-directed behaviors.

And from Results:

> None of the areas selected for study (3) exhibited an OEF significantly different from the
> hemispheric mean (compare Figs. 1 and 4). ... By our definition, therefore, none of these
> areas was activated in subjects who were awake but resting quietly with their eyes closed

**The paper contains no percentage figure at all about task-evoked versus baseline energy.**
Confirmed by reading the whole text. So it cannot be cited for the "<5%" claim.

**The correction.** It is *not* fair to call "the DMN is highly active at rest" a pure
press-release invention. The paper reports, and explicitly argues for, tonic activity and high
resting metabolic cost in the core midline nodes:

> Although none of the areas selected for study appear to be activated by our definition, two
> areas in both groups (i.e., M31/7 and M10) have both resting CBF and CMRO2 significantly above
> the global mean

> We posit that areas decreasing their activity in this manner may be tonically active in the
> baseline state, as distinguished from areas that are transiently activated in support of
> varying goal-directed activities.

> Thus, the posterior cingulate cortex and adjacent precuneus can be posited as a tonically
> active region of the brain that may continuously gather information about the world around,
> and possibly within, us. It would appear to be a default activity of the brain with rather
> obvious evolutionary significance.

> We wonder whether the exceptionally high metabolic rate exhibited by the posterior cingulate
> and precuneus adds to their vulnerability

So the accurate reading is: the paper's *method of identification* is task-induced decreases
from an OEF-defined baseline, and by the paper's own operational definition none of these
regions is "activated" at rest. But the paper *separately* reports that posterior
cingulate/precuneus and medial prefrontal cortex have resting blood flow and oxygen consumption
significantly above the whole-brain mean, and argues they are tonically active. "Highly active
at rest" is a loose paraphrase of a real, stated finding, not a fabrication — what is a
simplification is treating "high activity at rest" as the paper's *criterion* for the default
mode network, which it is not.

### Which source for which sentence

- **"The brain's resting energy consumption dwarfs the additional cost of any given task;
  task-related increases are often under 5% of ongoing baseline activity."** → cite
  **Raichle 2015, *Phil Trans R Soc B* 370: 20140172**, which states the figure in so many
  words and is open access so readers can check it. Cite **Raichle & Mintun 2006** alongside it
  as the review Raichle himself cites for the figure — but do not put the "<5%" in quotation
  marks against the 2006 review, because that sentence was not readable in this pass.
- **"The brain is ~2% of body weight but ~20% of energy consumed."** → Raichle 2015 (same
  paragraph), or Raichle et al. 2001, which states the same figures in its Background section.
- **"Intrinsic, ongoing activity is the largest fraction of the brain's functional activity."**
  → Raichle & Mintun 2006 abstract, quotable verbatim.
- **"There is an organised default mode of brain function, defined against a measurable
  baseline, that is suspended during goal-directed tasks."** → Raichle et al. 2001.
- Do **not** cite Raichle et al. 2001 for any percentage.

## SLOT C-26 — endogenous activity

### Item 1 — Berkes, P., Orbán, G., Lengyel, M. & Fiser, J., "Spontaneous cortical activity reveals hallmarks of an optimal internal model of the environment", *Science* 331(6013): 83–87 (2011), DOI 10.1126/science.1195870 — **CONFIRMED**

Read in full. Bibliographic details confirmed exactly as cited (PMID 21212356). The version read
is the NIH-deposited author accepted manuscript at PMC3065813 (the publisher's version of record
at Science is paywalled); the manuscript carries the standard Science accepted-version notice.
Its abstract matches the published abstract returned by Europe PMC except for trivial copy-edits
("While behavioral studies demonstrated" → "Although behavioral studies have demonstrated"). All
quotes below are from the accepted manuscript.

Central finding, verbatim (abstract):

> To test this prediction, we analyzed visual cortical activity of awake ferrets during
> development. Similarity between spontaneous and evoked activities increased with age and was
> specific to responses evoked by natural scenes. This demonstrates the progressive adaptation
> of internal models to the statistics of natural stimuli at the neural level.

The authors' own one-sentence summary, verbatim:

> One-sentence summary: The relation between spontaneous activity and activity evoked by natural
> stimuli in the primary visual cortex reveals that the cortical circuit progressively adapts its
> internal model to the statistical structure of the environment.

**What converges to what:** the distribution of *spontaneous* multi-neural activity patterns (SA,
recorded in darkness) converges on the *average distribution of activity evoked* by natural-scene
movies (aEA). Theoretically, SA is identified with the prior of an internal model and average EA
with the average posterior; the paper's own framing:

> the posterior represented by SA converges to the prior distribution

> The divergence between aEA and SA decreased with age (Fig. 2B-C, Spearman's, ρ = −0.70,
> p < 0.004) and the two distributions were not significantly different in mature animals

**Species and area:** awake, freely viewing **ferrets**, **primary visual cortex (V1)**; multiunit
spiking recorded on 16 electrodes, binned at 2 ms.

**Developmental timescale:** four stages, n = 16 animals total —

> at four different developmental stages: after eye opening at postnatal day (P) 29-30, after the
> maturation of orientation tuning and long-range horizontal connections at P44-45 (18), and in
> two groups of mature animals at P83-90 and P129-151

**Specificity to natural scenes** (this is the part the blog must not drop, since it is what makes
the result about a *model of the environment* rather than generic drift):

> in the oldest age group SA was significantly better matched to neural activity evoked by natural
> images than that evoked by the two artificial stimulus ensembles (Fig. 4A-B, movie vs. noise:
> m = 16.47, p < 0.05; movie vs. grating: m = 943.07, p < 0.002)

Conclusion, verbatim:

> Our results suggest that V1 implements an internal model that is adapted gradually during
> development to the statistical structure of the natural visual environment and that SA reflects
> prior expectations of this internal model.

Caveat worth one clause in the blog if it leans hard on this: the authors explicitly do not claim
the adaptation is experience-driven —

> While these findings do not address the degree to which statistical adaptation in the cortex is
> driven by visual experience or by developmental programs

### Item 2 — Clark, A., "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36(3): 181–204 (2013), DOI 10.1017/S0140525X12000477 — **CONFIRMED (previous judgement correct)**

Bibliographic details confirmed exactly as cited (PMID 23663408; published online 10 May 2013).
Read beyond the abstract: the full text is bronze open access at Cambridge Core and was read
there, plus the verbatim abstract from Europe PMC.

Abstract, verbatim:

> Brains, it has recently been argued, are essentially prediction machines. They are bundles of
> cells that support perception and action by constantly attempting to match incoming sensory
> inputs with top-down expectations or predictions. This is achieved using a hierarchical
> generative model that aims to minimize prediction error within a bidirectional cascade of
> cortical processing. Such accounts offer a unifying model of perception and action, illuminate
> the functional role of attention, and may neatly capture the special contribution of cortical
> processing to adaptive success. This target article critically examines this "hierarchical
> prediction machine" approach, concluding that it offers the best clue yet to the shape of a
> unified science of mind and action. Sections 1 and 2 lay out the key elements and implications
> of the approach. Section 3 explores a variety of pitfalls and challenges, spanning the
> evidential, the methodological, and the more properly conceptual. The paper ends (sections 4
> and 5) by asking how such approaches might impact our more general vision of mind, experience,
> and agency.

**The previous pass's judgement is CONFIRMED.** The paper's subject is continuous prediction
*against incoming input* — "constantly attempting to match incoming sensory inputs with top-down
expectations" — not structured activity in the *absence* of task input. A full-text search of the
Cambridge Core version returns **zero occurrences** of "spontaneous", "ongoing activity",
"resting state", "endogenous" and "restless". Opening line of the body confirms the framing:

> one of the brain's key tricks, it now seems, is to implement dumb processes that correct a
> certain kind of error: error in the multi-layered prediction of input

One genuine nuance in Clark's favour, §4.3, which is the closest the paper comes to
input-independent activity — but it is about imagery, not spontaneous activity:

> The practical upshot is that such systems, simply as part and parcel of learning to perceive,
> develop the ability to self-generate perception-like states from the top down, by driving the
> lower populations into the predicted patterns.

> There thus emerges a rather deep connection between perception and the potential for
> self-generated forms of mental imagery

So Clark supports "cortex is always running a top-down generative model, and can drive lower
levels into predicted patterns without the corresponding stimulus". He does not supply evidence
for structured activity in the absence of task input; Berkes et al. does.

Confirmed from the Cambridge Core record: this is a BBS target article published with **30 peer

## SLOT C-31 — grounding

### Item 1 — Harnad, S., "Language writ large: LLMs, ChatGPT, meaning, and understanding", *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence* 7: 1490698, DOI 10.3389/frai.2024.1490698 — **CONFIRMED**

Read in full (Europe PMC full text, PMC11861094; open access CC-BY). Bibliographic record:

- Published title: **"Language writ large: LLMs, ChatGPT, meaning, and understanding"** — confirmed.
- Author: Stevan Harnad, Department of Psychology, University of Montreal (correspondence address
  harnad@soton.ac.uk).
- Journal: *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence*, volume 7, article 1490698. PMID 40013231.
- Dates: **Received 3 September 2024; Accepted 20 December 2024; Published 12 February 2025.**
  (The DOI string carries 2024 because of the Frontiers collection year; the publication date is
  2025. Citing it as 2025 is correct.)
- Peer reviewed: Edited by Tony Veale (University College Dublin); reviewed by Tim Fernando
  (Trinity College Dublin) and Masao Yokota (Fukuoka Institute of Technology).

**Format — CONFIRMED, it is a dialogue with ChatGPT-4.** Harnad says so in the abstract:

> The exposition will be in the form of a dialogue with ChatGPT-4.

Fair one-sentence description for a blog reader: *The paper is written as a transcript of
Harnad's own extended conversation with ChatGPT-4, with Harnad ("SH") setting out the argument
turn by turn and GPT-4 summarising, elaborating or agreeing — so the analysis is Harnad's, while
a large share of the word count is the model's own output, prepared for talks he gave at MILA and
UQÀM.*

**Practical warning for quoting:** most of the article's text is GPT-4's replies. Quote only the
"SH:" turns and the abstract when attributing a view to Harnad.

**What LLMs lack (Harnad's own words).** The abstract:

> These biases are inherent in the nature of language itself, at the LLM scale, and they are
> closely linked to what it is that ChatGPT lacks, which is direct sensorimotor grounding to
> connect its words to their referents and its propositions to their meanings.

And the sharpest statement, in his own dialogue turn:

> But this is with the fundamental difference that for you, GPT—the "teacher," the verbal
> explainer—your own explanation is not "indirectly" grounded: it is not grounded at all!

> You lack grounding, just as a dictionary does. A dictionary's words are all parasitic on the
> grounding of the lexicographers who wrote it—and your words are parasitic on the grounding of
> the authors of your LLM database.

Also usable, more compact:

> an LLM super-dictionary, like an ordinary dictionary, would continue to be an ungrounded
> database, hence you, GPT are only capable of providing indirect grounding to grounded human
> heads, while yourself remaining completely ungrounded, either directly or indirectly.

**What he concedes LLMs nonetheless achieve — he is explicitly surprised.** Abstract:

> However, none of us can say (hand on heart) that we are not surprised by what ChatGPT has
> proved to be able to do with these resources. This has even driven some of us to conclude that
> ChatGPT actually understands. It is not true that it understands. But it is also not true that
> we understand how it can do what it can do.

Introduction:

> Large Language Models (LLMs; Pavlick, 2023) such as ChatGPT have surprised both researchers and
> the public by demonstrating capabilities that resemble human understanding.

His own dialogue turns, twice more:

> This is that persisting puzzle about your remarkable know-how, no matter how well your designers
> know exactly what you are doing to give you the capacity to do what you can do, despite your
> lacking grounding, and despite your lacking any possibility of gaining grounding

> Some of these might be generating some constraints and biases that explain some of the uncanny
> success of GPT in making and conveying sense. I think they tell us something not only about the
> power of Large Language Models but also about the power of natural language itself.

He also refers in passing to "GPT's (considerable and surprising) performance capacity". So the
blog is right that he is not a flat sceptic: his position is *LLMs definitely do not understand,
and we definitely do not understand how they do as well as they do* — and the whole paper is an
attempt to explain the gap with six conjectured "benign biases" inherent in language at LLM scale.

### Item 2 — Harnad, S., "The Symbol Grounding Problem", *Physica D* 42: 335–346 (1990), DOI 10.1016/0167-2789(90)90087-6 — **CONFIRMED**

Bibliographic details confirmed. Read in full at the author's own posting
(users.ecs.soton.ac.uk/harnad/Papers/Harnad/harnad90.sgproblem.html), which carries the citation
line in its own header:

> Harnad, S. (1990) The Symbol Grounding Problem. Physica D 42: 335-346.

Cross-checked against the arXiv deposit (cs/9906002), whose journal-ref line gives the same
"Physica D 42: 335-346".

Statement of the problem, from the abstract, verbatim:

> This paper describes the "symbol grounding problem": How can the semantic interpretation of a
> formal symbol system be made intrinsic to the system, rather than just parasitic on the meanings
> in our heads? How can the meanings of the meaningless symbol tokens, manipulated solely on the
> basis of their (arbitrary) shapes, be grounded in anything but other meaningless symbols? The
> problem is analogous to trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese/Chinese dictionary alone.

And the statement in the body (§2.2, "The Chinese/Chinese Dictionary-Go-Round"), verbatim:

> Suppose you had to learn Chinese as a first language and the only source of information you had
> was a Chinese/Chinese dictionary! This is more like the actual task faced by a purely symbolic
> model of the mind: How can you ever get off the symbol/symbol merry-go-round? How is symbol
> meaning to be grounded in something other than just more meaningless symbols? This is the symbol
> grounding problem.

His proposed solution, for context (abstract):

> Symbolic representations must be grounded bottom-up in nonsymbolic representations of two kinds:
> (1) "iconic representations", which are analogs of the proximal sensory projections of distal
> objects and events, and (2) "categorical representations", which are learned and innate
> feature-detectors that pick out the invariant features of object and event categories from their
> sensory projections.

### Damasio, A. & Carvalho, G.B., "The nature of feelings: evolutionary and neurobiological origins", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 14: 143–152 (2013), DOI 10.1038/nrn3403 — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**

Bibliographic record confirmed: *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* **14**(2): 143–152, published
18 January 2013, PMID 23329161. Authors at the Brain and Creativity Institute, University of

> Feelings are mental experiences of body states. They signify physiological need (for example,

**Central claim, as stated in the abstract:** feelings are mental experiences of body states that

## Per-slot verdicts

**SLOT C-25 — Yes, the claim can be made honestly, with the citation changed.** The "<5% of
ongoing baseline" figure is real, is Raichle's, and was read verbatim — but in Raichle 2015
(*Phil Trans R Soc B* 370: 20140172, open access), which cites Raichle & Mintun 2006 for it.
Suggested wording: *"The brain runs on roughly 20% of the body's energy budget for 2% of its
weight, and the extra energy a task demands is small against that: 'the additional energy
consumption associated with changes in brain activity is remarkably small, often less than 5% of
the baseline level of activity' (Raichle 2015, citing Raichle & Mintun 2006)."* Cite Raichle et
al. 2001 only for the default-mode/baseline claim, never for a percentage, and if the blog says
the DMN is "active at rest", say *"metabolically costly and, the authors argue, tonically active
at rest — identified not by switching on during tasks but by switching down"*, which is both
quotable and accurate.

**SLOT C-26 — Yes for Berkes; no for Clark as evidence, yes for Clark as framing.** Berkes et al.
2011 fully supports "cortex generates structured activity without task input, and that structure
is not arbitrary — in ferret V1 it converges over development onto the statistics of natural
scenes specifically". Clark 2013 should be cited for the predictive-processing *framework*
(continuous top-down prediction of incoming input, and the capacity to self-generate
perception-like states from the top down), not as evidence for input-free activity. Suggested
softening for Clark: *"Andy Clark's predictive-processing synthesis — a BBS target article
published with thirty peer commentaries, including sharp ones from Anderson and Chemero and from
Rasmussen and Eliasmith — frames cortex as constantly predicting its own input rather than
waiting on it."*

**SLOT C-31 — Yes, both items, and Harnad can be cited fairly rather than as a flat sceptic.**
Title, 2025 publication date, dialogue-with-GPT-4 format, the grounding claim and the concession
of surprise are all confirmed verbatim. The only discipline the blog needs is to quote the "SH:"
turns and the abstract, not GPT-4's replies, and to describe the format honestly in the same
sentence as the first quote. The 1990 Physica D citation and the canonical statement of the
problem are confirmed exactly as given.

**SLOT C-30 — Partially; soften or read the body.** The bibliographic record and the central
claim are confirmed from the abstract, but the article is paywalled and no homeostasis sentence
could be read. Either drop the quotation marks — *"Damasio and Carvalho argue that feelings are
mental experiences of body states and 'constitute a crucial component of the mechanisms of life
regulation, from simple to complex'"* — or get library access to the body before attributing the
word "homeostasis" to them in quotes.
