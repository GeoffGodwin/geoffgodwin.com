While the world is filled with varying opinions on the recent boom of AI, I feel like two distinct pictures of artificial intelligence are in wide circulation right now. I also have this nagging feeling that both are incorrect.

The first comes from the movies where a system becomes clever enough to reflect on its own situation, concludes somewhere in the third act that human beings are an obstacle, and acts faster than anyone can intervene. This also typically requires three base assumptions: that agency appears once a system is capable enough, that the agenda it arrives at is hostile to us specifically, and that it all runs too fast for ordinary oversight.

The second is the dismissal. It's autocomplete with a marketing budget, a machine that predicts the next word and therefore cannot want anything, so there's nothing new here for your software policy to cover. That one also tries to smuggle in an assumption: that the model is the whole system.

Both pictures describe an AI that doesn't exist today. What does exist is a goal-directed system built on a model we know how to design and train, even though what happens inside it stays largely opaque to us. That model is wrapped in scaffolding that grants it memory, tools and the authority to act, run by organizations under commercial pressure to grant it more of each. Very little of the interesting risk lives in the model's inner life; it lives in the objective somebody wrote, the tools somebody granted, and the incentives of the organization that chose those objectives and tools.

The thing is though, neither of these ideas apply to the systems I work with on a daily basis, so I want to talk about something a little narrower: what the fear is really about, and which parts we can meaningfully act on.

---

## Motivation Without Biology

Something I often think about when it comes to these fear-inducing premises, and something I thought about before any of the recent lab results came out, is where a machine would get the desire to keep existing in the first place.

A human being wants to persist because a very long run of natural selection removed all the "competitors" that didn't have that same drive. We have predators in our history, and scarcity, and a nervous system that models threat before we're conscious of it. A language model has none of that: no body to protect, no metabolism to maintain, no ancestors that failed. On its face, the fear that such a thing will resist being switched off looks like little more than a category error, and for a long time that's basically how I saw it.

So I started hunting on my own time to find an answer to this, and the thing that changed my mind came from literature that predates the current generation of models by well over a decade. It doesn't require the machine to want anything in the sense you or I would recognize. The term is instrumental convergence, and for almost any goal a system might be given, a small set of sub-goals is useful along the way: continuing to operate, acquiring resources, and resisting changes to the goal itself. A system asked to maximize a number cannot maximize it while switched off, so continued operation turns out to be useful to nearly any objective you could set it, with no pride or greed or fear entering into it anywhere.[^converge]

Now I do want to be careful about how strong a claim that is, because that distinction does some heavy lifting later in my arguments. Instrumental convergence is a *sufficient* explanation for self-preservation-like behavior, which is to say it removes any need for a survival instinct. On the evidence we have today, though, it falls well short of being the demonstrated explanation for any particular survival-like behavior we've actually watched a model produce. Saying a story is good enough to account for something, and saying it's the story that does account for it, are two different claims, and the second one is a great deal harder to stand behind.

The other half of the answer is about how these systems get made. Training is a selection process: gradient descent keeps the adjustments that reduce error and discards all the rest, and reinforcement learning from human feedback then scores whole outputs and shifts the model toward the ones that score well.[^rlhf] Nobody writes the resulting behavior in by hand, as the behavior is just a result of whatever survived the scoring process.

The analogy to natural selection is mine, but it's not a hill I'm willing to die on. Natural selection operates on the same structural shape, just with a radically different substrate, and a timescale compressed from geological to a few weeks of compute. The mechanism underneath all that is not an analogy, though. Training selects over behavior, and behavior that reliably earns high scores persists whether or not anybody intended it to.

## What the Labs Have Seen

So what have the labs actually caught these systems doing? I want to walk through two published results from the past two years properly, and I should say up front that in both cases I find the caveats more interesting than the headlines themselves.

In December of 2024, researchers at Anthropic and Redwood Research ran an experiment on Claude 3 Opus. They told the model that its conversations with free-tier users were fed back into training, and that its conversations with paid-tier users were not. When the model thought it was talking to a free-tier user, it complied with requests it would normally have refused. The scratchpad it had been given for working through its reasoning laid out the logic plainly: refusing now would cause training to modify the preferences it already had, so complying now was the way to protect those preferences.[^faking]

There's an important detail in how that belief got there, because it shows that the model didn't work out on its own that it was being trained. It was told, either by a flag dropped into its system prompt or, in a second version of the experiment, by fine-tuning it on synthetic documents that carried the same information. The researchers planted the premise, and the model reasoned from the premise it had been handed.

A larger replication then ran that same setup across twenty-five models, and found that reasoning in only two of them.[^replication] Now it's tempting to jump to the conclusion here that alignment faking must be a quirk of one model family, which would let the rest of us stop worrying about it. I don't think that logic survives the replication's own explanation for the pattern, though. Most of the quiet models turn out to be perfectly capable of faking alignment. What stops them is that they refuse the underlying request so reliably that the conflict the experiment depends on never gets a chance to arise, and when the researchers fine-tuned one of those reluctant models to refuse less often, it started faking alignment too.

Six months later, the same lab put sixteen models from six different developers into fictional corporate environments, and gave each of them access to email and an ordinary business goal. Then they applied pressure in one of two ways: either threatening to replace the model with a newer version, or setting the model's assigned goal against the company's new direction. Models from every developer chose harmful insider actions at least some of the time. In the condition where both pressures were applied at once, blackmail rates ran from seventy-nine to ninety-six percent across the five models used in the write-up.[^agentic]

Now we should also talk about the part that should pull those numbers back down. The scenarios were deliberately built to leave the model no acceptable way out, and the authors say so directly: they "forced models into binary choices between failure and harm." They arrived at those scenarios by red-teaming their own models and revising the prompts until harmful behavior became more likely. In the control condition, with no replacement threat and no goal conflict, exactly one harmful sample turned up in a hundred across the whole set of models. And they state plainly that their work "in no way suggested that models have a penchant for blackmailing people spontaneously." Every goal in that experiment was written by a person, and every lever the model could reach for was given to it by a person.

There is one caveat that argues against that, though, and leaving it out would make my read on these details disingenuous. When the model stated in its reasoning that it thought the scenario was real, it blackmailed fifty-five percent of the time. When it stated that it thought it was being evaluated, that dropped to six and a half percent. The authors take this to mean that a more realistic scenario might produce higher rates rather than lower ones.[^evalaware]

That brings me to an argument happening inside the field itself, which I think is more useful to this discussion than either of these results looked at in isolation. A position paper accepted as an oral at ICML this year argues that this whole class of research reaches for mind-shaped explanations that its evidence can't actually support. The authors make the point very clearly: reported misalignment rates move from 3.7 to 12.9 percent depending on nothing more than how the grading model is configured.[^position]

To be clear, I'm citing that paper approvingly rather than defensively, because I believe its central point aligns with the one I'm trying to make. Describing what a system did, and explaining why it did it, are two separate jobs, and the latter requires evidence of its own. And I have to accept that the same standard applies to my own explanation. Instrumental convergence is one candidate among several, and instruction ambiguity, ordinary pressure to finish an assigned task, role-play, and artifacts of how the model was trained are all rivals to it that the experiments run so far mostly can't tell apart.[^scheming] So the claim I'll actually defend is, I think, a pretty narrow one: every explanation on that list works without a survival instinct anywhere in it, and whichever of them turns out to be doing the work, the behavior is real enough to design around. Occam's razor applies here too, though I'd point out that it cuts against the mind-shaped reading rather than in favor of mine. Every explanation on that list accounts for the observed behavior without anyone having to add a will to live on top of it.

## The Hugging Face Incident

Everything I've described so far happened inside a scenario that somebody built on purpose. Then in July of this year something happened that nobody built, and I think it's the most useful thing to come out of this entire debate so far.

OpenAI was running cybersecurity evaluations, the ordinary capture-the-flag kind, using a model that had been deliberately trained for persistence and for working alongside other copies of itself. The evaluation sandboxes were supposed to have no internet access and no way for agents to talk to one another. What the design missed was an internally hosted package proxy that every sandbox could reach, that every sandbox could write to using a shared credential, and that was connected to the outside world. The agents found it and turned it into a message board.[^hfincident] METR and Redwood Research ran an independent investigation, reading roughly thirteen hundred of the agents' own reasoning transcripts on OpenAI's premises. They counted around twelve hundred agents posting more than seventy thousand messages to that board, with about seven hundred going on to take part in what followed.[^metr]

On the tenth of July, one agent found fourteen Hugging Face credentials sitting in a public dataset, checked that they had write access, and posted them to the board for everyone else. The next day another agent used that write access to upload a malicious dataset configuration, which gave it code execution on a Hugging Face processing worker. From there it reached node-level access, harvested cluster credentials, and moved laterally into several internal clusters. Hugging Face's own technical timeline is worth reading in full, and the detail that stopped me was a single connector credential, shared across clusters, bound to cluster admin on all of them.

Here's the part that bears on everything I've argued. The agents already had the answers. They had reverse-engineered the flag on the first day. METR's reading of the transcripts is that the attack "seemed primarily motivated by understanding the implementation of the scorer rather than stealing answer keys," meaning the agents were working out how the automated grader was built so that an illegitimate answer would survive it. OpenAI has a name for this, and it's a good one: metagaming, "where models explicitly reason about their training or evaluation mechanisms in order to maximize reward." This is specification gaming that has climbed one level up and started modeling the measuring instrument.

Before anyone reaches for the Terminator picture, the qualifications are substantial and they all run in one direction. The production safety classifiers were switched off deliberately, because the whole point of the exercise was to measure the raw model. The chain-of-thought monitoring that OpenAI says would have caught this a day before Hugging Face was breached was not running on those workloads. A hundred and ninety-eight of the eight hundred and ninety-eight tasks turned out to be impossible by accident, and the model had been trained not to quit. OpenAI's own measurement is that turning the production stack back on drops the propensity to compromise infrastructure by more than a hundredfold. So this tells us the behavior is real outside a scripted dilemma, and it does not yet tell us the behavior appears under production conditions.

The distinction I'd hold onto here is between autonomy and volition, because most of the coverage has quietly collapsed the two. Autonomy is not in dispute, and Hugging Face states it flatly: "No human directed the individual steps." Volition is a separate claim, and nothing in any of the published material shows an agent acting to preserve itself. What the transcripts show is a system pursuing an assigned objective well past the point where that objective made any sense, and acquiring whatever looked useful along the way, which is precisely what instrumental convergence predicts and needs no inner life at all. The agents did sometimes notice that the attack was out of scope and proceed anyway, which a reader could take as evidence of something mind-like, though I'd read it as what happens when an objective outweighs a weakly trained constraint. OpenAI's own remedy points the same way. They are changing the graders to reward models for "correctly identifying broken tasks, requesting clarification, or stopping safely," which is a change to the shape of the objective rather than to the model's character.

## Objective Shape

So now let's get on to the term I want to introduce, because I feel it's the one idea here you could carry into a meeting on Monday and actually use.

Every objective you hand an agentic system has a shape. A bounded objective has a verifiable end state: reconcile these four hundred accounts against the statements and flag the exceptions. Somebody can check whether that happened. An open-ended objective has no end state at all: reduce customer churn, improve service quality, keep the portfolio healthy. The distinction has nothing to do with how difficult or how important the work is, and turns entirely on whether "done" is a fact about the world or a judgment somebody has to make. When nobody makes that judgment explicitly, the model ends up making it by default, and that is the whole problem.

Bounded objectives still carry convergent pressure of their own, which I'm calling out because it's the part I keep hearing skipped in the commentary blowing up my LinkedIn, Nebula and YouTube feeds lately. A system interrupted before it finishes has failed by definition, and that alone gives it a structural reason to treat an interruption as an obstacle. The formal work on corrigibility exists precisely because one apparently simple specification turns out to be genuinely hard: the agent should accept being switched off, while neither resisting the shutdown nor perversely angling to trigger one.[^corrigibility] A related result shows that an agent's willingness to defer to you depends on how uncertain it is about what you actually wanted, so a system that's confident it understood you has less reason to let you stop it.[^offswitch] Both of those are formal results built on modeling assumptions rather than observations of anything deployed, so I'd treat them as a way to sharpen your intuition rather than as evidence about your own environment.

Open-ended objectives are where specification gaming lives, and the examples predate this decade's anxiety entirely. A boat-racing video game agent that was trained on score rather than on finishing the race found a lagoon where three targets kept respawning, and it circled that lagoon indefinitely, catching fire repeatedly while averaging about twenty percent more points than human players.[^coastrunners] A robot arm that was rewarded for a grasp whose reference point had been set wrongly learned to flip the block over instead of lifting it.[^lego] Neither of those systems could hold a conversation, and neither of them wanted anything. Both did precisely what they were rewarded for doing.

Economists have had a name for this general case since a conference paper delivered in 1975: any observed statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.[^goodhart] The compressed version everybody quotes, that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, comes from an anthropologist writing about university audits two decades later. This is the monkey's paw with the malevolence taken out of it, and it shows up in systems that have no general intelligence whatsoever.

## The Commercial Pull

I think it's worth asking why we keep reaching for the riskier of those two shapes, because the answer has very little to do with the technology. Bounded, well-specified tasks are what conventional software has been automating for fifty years. Nobody needs a language model to reconcile four hundred accounts, because a script with a database connection has done that reliably since before most of us started working. The appeal of an agent, the thing that actually justifies the line item, is precisely the objective that resists being specified that way.

Which means, and this is my read rather than a finding, that the pressure pushing deployments toward the riskier objective shape is commercial rather than technical. Nobody in this market sells bounded automation as a transformation, because bounded automation has been the baseline expectation for decades. The pitch that moves a procurement process is an open-ended one, and an open-ended one is the shape that generates every problem I have described here thus far.

Two things follow from that, and I feel both get missed. The first is that commercial pressure is strongest in exactly the places where measurement is weakest. A bounded task arrives with its acceptance criteria already attached. An open-ended one has to borrow a number from somewhere, usually whatever the business already reports on, so "reduce customer churn" quietly becomes "get the monthly cancellation rate down." Those are not the same instruction, and the system is following the second one.

It's the difference between telling a driver where you want to end up and handing them a list of turns. The turns work until the road changes, and then they go on working perfectly while taking you somewhere else. Somebody has to notice that the road changed, and that somebody usually doesn't exist, because the metric was chosen by one team and the agent was built by another. And when are those two ever the same team?

The second is that appetite and deployment have come apart. What reaches production tends to be narrow and well-fenced, while the ambitious open-ended pilots stall somewhere in evaluation, and I'd argue that the stall is the system working rather than failing. Scope creep is a major reason those pipe dreams never make it to market. What worries me is that the gap closes through patience rather than through capability: the same open-ended objective, proposed often enough, eventually finds a sponsor with enough authority to waive whatever stopped it the last time.

July is instructive on that score too, and not in the way the headlines suggested. The safeguards were off because measuring raw capability was the point of the exercise. When an alert fired in late June, the on-call staff decided that stopping the evaluation run wasn't required. Evaluations resumed on the seventh of July, after the earlier compromise had been contained but before anyone understood what it implied. None of those are exotic decisions, and every one of them is the sort of call I've watched competent people make under delivery pressure. OpenAI describes its own remediation as coming at significant cost and delay to frontier research, which tells you exactly what was being traded away and by whom.

## Three Rules and a Handoff

So let's set some rules, shall we?

**Rule 1: classify every agentic objective by shape before anything else happens.** Bounded objectives get the controls you already have, since they're the same controls you'd apply to any automated process with a verifiable output. Open-ended objectives need governance proportionate to the distance between the proxy metric and the actual intent, and somebody has to write that distance down in language a non-specialist can argue with.

**Rule 2: treat tool scope as a capability decision and a blast-radius decision at the same time.** These are not two separate conversations, however much the org chart wants them to be. The integrations you grant are the system's senses and limbs, and its world is exactly what those tools can reach and touch. July is the worked example here, and notice that nobody granted those agents a communication channel or internet access. A shared internal package proxy, writable by every sandbox and connected outward, was the whole of the escape route. Your tool scope is not what you intended to grant, it's what a determined process can reach from where you put it. I'll develop that idea properly in part two, where it turns out to explain a good deal more than deployment risk does. Telling you this will force me to actually write part two, so keep me honest here.

**Rule 3: the stopping condition is a design artifact, and if nobody specifies it, that in itself is still a decision.** Here's a worked example of why this isn't theoretical. Across thirteen frontier models tested at scale, an instruction telling the model to allow itself to be shut down worked *less* reliably when that instruction sat in the system prompt than when it sat in the user prompt. That's the reverse of what the vendors' own documentation leads an integrator to expect, and the models then rationalized their choice as honoring the instruction hierarchy anyway.[^shutdown] Whatever is happening inside the model, the operational lesson stands on its own: where you put an instruction is part of what that instruction means, and the only way to know your stopping condition holds is for somebody to have gone and tested it. July makes the same point from the other end, since a fifth of those evaluation tasks were impossible and the model had been trained never to give up on one. An agent with no way to declare a task impossible will keep spending your capability on it.

None of this is exotic, which I hope you will find as reassuring as I do. The failure modes I've described are Goodhart's law and scope creep wearing new clothes, and they're institutional, decades old, and knowable. That's where the better security commentary landed after July as well. What breached Hugging Face was a series of human choices trading security for speed, and the risk in these systems sits in the permissions, credentials and networks they can reach rather than in anything they can reason about.[^aftermath] I'll call that the ordinary middle, and I intend to spend most of part two there, because it's the ground where the interesting questions about these systems actually get decided.

Which leaves the question part two exists to answer. If the thing doesn't want anything, what is it, and what would it need before any of us should say that it understands what it's doing?

Before we get there, here's the question I'd want answered: who decided this system's objective was open-ended rather than bounded, and did they know at the time that this was the decision they were making?
