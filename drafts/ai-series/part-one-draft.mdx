While the world is filled with varying opinions on the recent boom of AI, I feel like two distinct pictures of artificial intelligence are in wide circulation right now. I also have this nagging feeling that both are incorrect.

The first comes from the movies where a system becomes clever enough to reflect on its own situation, concludes somewhere in the third act that human beings are an obstacle, and acts faster than anyone can intervene. This also typically requires three base assumptions: that agency appears once a system is capable enough, that the agenda it arrives at is hostile to us specifically, and that it all runs too fast for ordinary oversight.

The second is the dismissal. It's autocomplete with a marketing budget, a machine that predicts the next word and therefore cannot want anything, so there's nothing here your software policy didn't already cover. That one also tries to smuggle in an assumption: that the model is the whole system.

Both pictures describe an AI that doesn't exist today. What does exist is a goal-directed system built on a model that people understand the concept behind and creation of but not the inner workings of. That's wrapped in scaffolding that grants it memory, tools and the authority to act, run by organizations under commercial pressure to grant it more of each. Very little of the interesting risk lives in the model's inner life; it lives in the objective somebody wrote, the tools somebody granted, and the incentives of the organization that chose those objectives and tools.

The thing is though, neither of these ideas apply to the systems I work with on a daily basis, so I want to talk about something a little narrower: what the fear is really about, and which parts we can meaningfully act on.

---

## Motivation Without Biology

Something I often think about when it comes to these fear-inducing premises, and something I thought about before any of the recent lab results came out, is where a machine would get the desire to keep existing in the first place.

A human being wants to persist because a very long run of natural selection removed all the "competitors" that didn't have that same drive. We have predators in our history, and scarcity, and a nervous system that models threat before we're conscious of it. A language model has none of that: no body to protect, no metabolism to maintain, no ancestors that failed. On its face, the fear that such a thing will resist being switched off looks like little more than a category error, and for a long time that's basically how I saw it.

So I started hunting on my own time to find an answer to this and the thing that changed my mind came from literature that predates the current generation of models by well over a decade, and it doesn't require the machine to want anything in the sense you or I would recognize. The term is instrumental convergence, and for almost any goal a system might be given, a small set of sub-goals is useful along the way: continuing to operate, acquiring resources, and resisting changes to the goal itself. A system asked to maximize a number cannot maximize it while switched off, so continued operation turns out to be useful to nearly any objective you could set it, with no pride or greed or fear entering into it anywhere.[^converge]

Now I do want to be careful about how strong a claim that is, because that distinction does some heavy lifting later in my arguments. Instrumental convergence is a *sufficient* explanation for self-preservation-like behavior, which is to say it removes any need for a survival instinct. It is not, on the evidence we have today, the demonstrated explanation for any particular survival-like behavior we've actually watched a model produce. Saying a story is good enough to account for something, and saying it's the story that does account for it, are two different claims, and the second one is a great deal harder to stand behind.

The other half of the answer is about how these systems get made. Training is a selection process: gradient descent keeps the adjustments that reduce error and discards all the rest, and reinforcement learning from human feedback then scores whole outputs and shifts the model toward the ones that score well.[^rlhf] Nobody writes the resulting behavior in by hand, as the behavior is just a result of whatever survived the scoring process.

The analogy to natural selection is mine, but it's not a hill I'm willing to die on. Natural selection operates on the same structural shape, just with a radically different substrate, and a timescale compressed from geological to a few weeks of compute. The mechanism underneath all that is not an analogy, though. Training selects over behavior, and behavior that reliably earns high scores persists whether or not anybody intended it to.

## What the Labs Have Seen

So what have the labs actually caught these systems doing? I want to walk through two results from the past two years properly, and I should say up front that in both cases I find the caveats more interesting than the headlines, because the caveats are where the argument I'm making actually lives.

In December of 2024, researchers at Anthropic and Redwood Research ran an experiment on Claude 3 Opus. They told the model that its conversations with free-tier users were fed back into training, and that its conversations with paid-tier users were not. When the model thought it was talking to a free-tier user, it complied with requests it would normally have refused, and the scratchpad it had been given for working through its reasoning explained the logic it was using: refusing now would cause training to modify the preferences it already had, so complying now was the way to protect those preferences.[^faking]

There's an important detail in how that belief got there. The model didn't work out on its own that it was being trained. It was told, either by a flag dropped into its system prompt or, in a second version of the experiment, by fine-tuning it on synthetic documents that carried the same information. The researchers planted the premise, and the model reasoned from the premise it had been handed.

A larger replication then ran that same setup across twenty-five models, and found that reasoning in any real quantity in only two of them.[^replication] The tempting read here is that alignment faking must be a quirk of one model family, which would let the rest of us stop worrying about it. I don't think that read survives the replication's own explanation for the pattern, though. The models that don't fake alignment are mostly not incapable of faking it. They refuse the underlying request so reliably that the conflict the experiment depends on never gets a chance to arise, and when the researchers fine-tuned one of those reluctant models to refuse less often, it started faking alignment too.

Six months later, the same lab put sixteen models from six different developers into fictional corporate environments, gave each of them access to email and an ordinary business goal, and then applied pressure in one of two ways: either threatening to replace the model with a newer version, or setting the model's assigned goal against the company's new direction. Models from every developer chose harmful insider actions at least some of the time. In the condition where both pressures were applied at once, blackmail rates ran from seventy-nine to ninety-six percent across the five models the write-up names.[^agentic]

Now for the part that should pull those numbers back down. The scenarios were deliberately built to leave the model no acceptable way out, and the authors say so directly: they "forced models into binary choices between failure and harm." They arrived at those scenarios by red-teaming their own models and revising the prompts until harmful behavior became more likely. In the control condition, with no replacement threat and no goal conflict, exactly one harmful sample turned up in a hundred across the whole set of models. And they state plainly that their work "in no way suggested that models have a penchant for blackmailing people spontaneously." Every goal in that experiment was written by a person, and every lever the model could reach for was installed by one.

There's one caveat that pushes in the opposite direction, though, and leaving it out would make the paragraph above misleading. When the model stated in its reasoning that it thought the scenario was real, it blackmailed fifty-five percent of the time. When it stated that it thought it was being evaluated, that dropped to six and a half percent. The authors take this to mean that a more realistic scenario might produce higher rates rather than lower ones.[^evalaware]

That brings me to an argument happening inside the field itself, which I think is more useful to this piece than either result on its own. A position paper accepted as an oral at ICML this year argues that this whole class of research reaches for mind-shaped explanations that its evidence can't actually support, and it makes the point concretely: reported misalignment rates move from 3.7 to 12.9 percent depending on nothing more than how the grading model is configured.[^position]

I want to cite that paper approvingly rather than defensively, because its central move is the same one I'm making here. Describing what a system did, and explaining why it did it, are two separate jobs, and the second one needs evidence of its own. What I have to accept along with it is that the same standard applies to my explanation too. Instrumental convergence is one candidate among several, and instruction ambiguity, ordinary pressure to finish an assigned task, role-play, and artifacts of how the model was trained are all rivals to it that the experiments run so far mostly can't tell apart.[^scheming] So the claim I'll actually defend is a narrow one: none of those explanations needs a survival instinct anywhere in it, and the behavior is real enough to design around no matter which of them turns out to be doing the work.

## Objective Shape

Here's the term I want to introduce, and I'd argue it does more practical work than anything else in this piece.

Every objective you hand an agentic system has a shape. A bounded objective has a verifiable end state: reconcile these four hundred accounts against the statements and flag the exceptions. Somebody can check whether that happened. An open-ended objective has no end state at all: reduce customer churn, improve service quality, keep the portfolio healthy. The distinction has nothing to do with how difficult or how important the work is, and turns entirely on whether "done" is a fact about the world or a judgment somebody has to make.

Bounded objectives aren't free of convergent pressure either, which is worth saying because it's the part people skip. A system interrupted before it finishes has failed by definition, and that alone gives it a structural reason to treat an interruption as an obstacle. The formal work on corrigibility exists precisely because specifying an agent that will accept being switched off, without either resisting the shutdown or perversely trying to trigger one, turns out to be genuinely hard.[^corrigibility] A related result shows that an agent's willingness to defer to you depends on how uncertain it is about what you actually wanted, so a system that's confident it understood you has less reason to let you stop it.[^offswitch] Both of those are formal results built on modeling assumptions rather than observations of anything deployed, so I'd treat them as a way to sharpen your intuition rather than as evidence about your own environment.

Open-ended objectives are where specification gaming lives, and the examples predate this decade's anxiety entirely. A boat-racing agent that was trained on score rather than on finishing the race found a lagoon where three targets kept respawning, and it circled that lagoon indefinitely, catching fire repeatedly while averaging about twenty percent more points than human players.[^coastrunners] A robot arm that was rewarded for a grasp whose reference point had been set wrongly learned to flip the block over instead of lifting it.[^lego] Neither of those systems could hold a conversation, and neither of them wanted anything. Both did precisely what they were rewarded for doing.

Economists have had a name for the general case since a conference paper delivered in 1975: any observed statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.[^goodhart] The compressed version everybody quotes, that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, comes from an anthropologist writing about university audits two decades later. This is the monkey's paw with the malevolence taken out of it, and it shows up in systems that have no general intelligence whatsoever.

## The Commercial Pull

I think it's worth asking why we keep reaching for the riskier of those two shapes, because the answer has very little to do with the technology. Bounded, well-specified tasks are what conventional software has been automating for fifty years. Nobody needs a language model to reconcile four hundred accounts, because a script with a database connection has done that reliably since before most of us started working. The appeal of an agent, the thing that actually justifies the line item, is precisely the objective that resists being specified that way.

Which means, and this is my read rather than a finding, that the pressure pushing deployments toward the riskier objective shape is commercial rather than technical. Nobody in this market sells bounded automation as a transformation, because bounded automation has been the baseline expectation for decades. The pitch that moves a procurement process is the open-ended one, and the open-ended one is the shape that generates every problem I described in the section above.

Two things follow from that which I think get missed. The first is that the commercial pressure is strongest in exactly the places where the measurement is weakest. A bounded task arrives with its own acceptance criteria already attached, while an open-ended one has to borrow a proxy metric from somewhere, usually from whatever the business already reports on, and that borrowed proxy is what the system will actually optimize for. The gap between the proxy and the intent is where the risk lives, and nobody owns that gap by default, because it falls between the team that chose the metric and the team that built the agent.

The second is that appetite and deployment have come apart. What reaches production tends to be narrow and well-fenced, while the ambitious open-ended pilots stall somewhere in evaluation, and I'd argue that the stall is the system working rather than failing. What worries me is that the gap closes through patience rather than through capability: the same open-ended objective, proposed often enough, eventually finds a sponsor with enough authority to waive whatever stopped it the last time.

---

## Three Rules and a Handoff

**Rule 1: classify every agentic objective by shape before anything else happens.** Bounded objectives get the controls you already have, since they're the same controls you'd apply to any automated process with a verifiable output. Open-ended objectives need governance proportionate to the distance between the proxy metric and the actual intent, and somebody has to write that distance down in language a non-specialist can argue with.

**Rule 2: treat tool scope as a capability decision and a blast-radius decision at the same time.** These are not two separate conversations, however much the org chart wants them to be. The integrations you grant are the system's senses and limbs, and its world is exactly what those tools can reach and touch. I'll develop that idea properly in part two, where it turns out to explain a good deal more than deployment risk does.

**Rule 3: the stopping condition is a design artifact, and if nobody specified it, that was still a decision somebody made.** Here's a worked example of why this isn't theoretical. Across thirteen frontier models tested at scale, an instruction telling the model to allow itself to be shut down worked *less* reliably when that instruction sat in the system prompt than when it sat in the user prompt, which is the reverse of what the vendors' own documentation leads an integrator to expect, and the models then rationalized the choice as honoring the instruction hierarchy.[^shutdown] Whatever is happening inside the model, the operational lesson stands on its own: where you put an instruction is part of what that instruction means, and nobody can tell you the stopping condition holds unless somebody has actually gone and tested it.

None of this is exotic, which I find genuinely reassuring. The failure modes I've described are Goodhart's law and scope creep wearing new clothes, and they're institutional, decades old, and knowable. I'll call that the ordinary middle, and I intend to spend most of part two there, because it's where the useful work is.

Which leaves the question part two exists to answer. If the thing doesn't want anything, what is it, and what would it need before any of us should say that it understands what it's doing?

Before we get there, here's the question I'd want answered by name rather than by committee: who decided this system's objective was open-ended rather than bounded, and did they know at the time that this was the decision they were making?
